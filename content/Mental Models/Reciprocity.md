## Definition
People feel obligated to return favors and treat others as they've been treated. When someone does something for you, you feel indebted to reciprocate.

## Key Insight
Reciprocity is hardwired into human psychology. It's how societies build trust and cooperation. But this instinct can be exploited—even small, unsolicited favors create a sense of obligation that often exceeds the value of the original favor.

## How to Apply
Understanding reciprocity:
- Give genuinely without expecting immediate return (builds long-term relationships)
- Recognize when reciprocity is being used to manipulate you
- Be generous, but set boundaries (you don't owe people for unsolicited favors)
- In negotiations, small concessions can trigger larger reciprocal concessions

## Real-World Example
A waiter brings you a mint with the check. Tips increase by 3%. Bring two mints? Tips increase by 14%. Bring one mint, walk away, then come back and say "For you nice folks, here's an extra mint"? Tips increase by 23%. The perceived gift creates obligation to reciprocate, even though you didn't ask for mints.

Charities send you address labels with a donation request. You didn't ask for labels, but now you feel slightly obligated to donate because they "gave" you something first. The labels cost them pennies but trigger reciprocity worth dollars.

Free samples at the grocery store: After eating a sample, you're more likely to buy the product even if you didn't particularly like it. The "gift" of the sample creates subtle obligation.

A colleague helps you with a project. Weeks later, they ask for help with something. You're far more likely to say yes because of reciprocity, even if their task is more burdensome than what they helped you with.

## Common Pitfalls
- Giving purely to manipulate (people sense this and it backfires)
- Feeling obligated to bad actors who give you unwanted "gifts"
- Not recognizing reciprocity in yourself (you're not immune)
- Being so guarded against manipulation that you reject genuine generosity
