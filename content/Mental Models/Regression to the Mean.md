## Definition
Extreme outcomes are likely to be followed by more moderate ones. Outlier performances tend to move back toward the average over time.

## Key Insight
Randomness creates extremes. When you see an exceptional result (very good or very bad), it's often partly due to luck. The next result will likely be closer to average because the luck component won't repeat. This isn't a force pulling things toward average—it's a statistical artifact we misinterpret as meaningful.

## How to Apply
When you see extreme performance (yours or others'):
- Ask: "How much of this was skill versus luck/randomness?"
- Expect the next result to be less extreme
- Don't over-reward or over-punish based on single outlier results
- Be skeptical of trends after extremely good or bad periods

## Real-World Example
A student scores 95% on a test (their usual average is 75%). Excited, their parents promise a reward for each future A grade. The next test they score 78%. The parents think the child stopped trying, but it's likely regression to the mean—the 95% included some lucky guesses.

Sports Illustrated famously has a "cover jinx"—athletes perform worse after appearing on the cover. The real explanation: athletes appear on the cover after exceptional performance (career highs often involving luck). Their next performance naturally regresses toward their actual average skill level.

A struggling salesperson has a terrible quarter (worst ever). Their manager threatens termination. Next quarter, performance improves. The manager credits the threat, but it was likely just regression to the mean.

## Common Pitfalls
- Attributing regression to mean to your interventions
- Punishing/rewarding people for statistical noise
- Expecting trends to continue indefinitely
