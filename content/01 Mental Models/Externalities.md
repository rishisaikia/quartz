## Definition
Costs or benefits of an activity that fall on parties not directly involved in the transaction. Negative externalities impose costs on outsiders without compensating them; positive externalities create benefits for outsiders who didn't pay for them.

## Key Insight
Markets work well when all costs and benefits are captured by the parties making decisions. When significant costs are external—borne by third parties, future generations, or the environment—private decision-makers systematically over-produce harmful activities and under-produce beneficial ones. The price signal is broken.

## How to Apply
Identifying externalities:
- Who bears costs not reflected in the transaction price?
- Who benefits without paying?
- What timescale are consequences operating on?

Correcting for them:
- Taxes or regulations can internalize negative externalities (carbon taxes, pollution caps)
- Subsidies can internalize positive externalities (education, vaccination)
- Property rights assignment can enable private negotiation (Coase theorem)
- When externalities are large enough, private markets require intervention

## Real-World Example
**Air pollution:** A factory produces goods cheaply by emitting pollution. The factory captures revenue; neighbors and the atmosphere bear the health and environmental costs. The factory has no incentive to reduce pollution—the cost is external. Result: too much pollution relative to the social optimum.

**Vaccination:** When you get vaccinated, you protect others who can't be vaccinated (herd immunity). You don't capture this benefit in your vaccination decision. Result: under-vaccination relative to what's socially optimal—a positive externality problem.

**Education:** An educated population benefits everyone through lower crime, higher productivity, civic participation. Individual families don't capture all these gains. Result: under-investment in education without public subsidy.

**Traffic:** Each driver decides whether to commute based on their time cost. They don't factor in the congestion they add for everyone else. Result: too much driving—each car makes everyone else slightly worse off.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming government should correct every externality (transaction costs and regulatory failures can be worse)
- Ignoring positive externalities and focusing only on negative ones
- Underestimating how large externalities can be relative to the private transaction
- Missing that externalities can be geographically or temporally distant from the transaction
