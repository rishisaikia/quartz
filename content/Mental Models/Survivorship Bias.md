## Definition
Focusing on the people or things that survived a selection process while overlooking those that did not, leading to false conclusions.

## Key Insight
Success stories are visible; failures disappear quietly. When you only study winners, you're learning from an incomplete and biased dataset. The graveyard of failures often holds more useful information.

## How to Apply
When analyzing success patterns, ask:
- "Who tried this and failed?"
- "What am I not seeing because it no longer exists?"
- "Am I only looking at winners?"

Study failures as actively as you study successes.

## Real-World Example
During World War II, the military analyzed returning bombers to see where they took damage. They wanted to add armor to the areas with the most bullet holes. Statistician Abraham Wald pointed out the flaw: they were only examining planes that survived. The bullet holes showed where a plane could be hit and still make it home. They should armor the untouched areas—because planes hit there didn't survive to be analyzed.

Similarly, studying successful entrepreneurs who dropped out of college ignores the millions who dropped out and failed. The dropout isn't the cause of success.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming you can spot survivorship bias in others but not yourself
- Over-correcting and only studying failures
- Dismissing all success stories as "just luck"
