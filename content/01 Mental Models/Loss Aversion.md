## Definition
People feel the pain of losing something roughly twice as intensely as the pleasure of gaining something of equal value. Losses loom larger than gains.

## Key Insight
We're not rational calculators weighing gains versus losses equally. Losing $100 hurts more than gaining $100 feels good. This asymmetry shapes our decisions in ways that often work against our interests, making us risk-averse when we should be bold and holding onto losses when we should let go.

## How to Apply
Recognize loss aversion in decisions:
- Are you holding something because you own it, not because it's valuable?
- Are you avoiding a change because you fear losing what you have?
- Would you choose differently if you didn't already possess it?
- Reframe decisions in terms of gains rather than losses

## Real-World Example
You own stock that's dropped 30%. You refuse to sell, thinking "I'll wait until it gets back to what I paid" even though better investments exist. You're anchored to avoiding the realized loss, even though the money is already gone. The question should be: "Is this the best place for my money now?" not "How do I avoid admitting I lost money?"

In retail: "Buy now, 50% off!" motivates less than "Don't miss out—save $50!" The second frames it as a loss you'll experience if you don't act.

People will drive 20 minutes to save $10 on a $20 item, but won't drive 20 minutes to save $10 on a $1,000 item—same $10, but in the first case it feels like avoiding a loss (50%) versus a trivial gain (1%).

## Common Pitfalls
- Becoming so aware of loss aversion you take no risks
- Holding losing positions too long
- Not distinguishing genuine protection from irrational fear
