## Definition
The intersection of what you love, what you're good at, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for. Finding your ikigai means finding work that satisfies all four dimensions.

## Key Insight
Optimizing for just one dimension leads to dissatisfaction. Love what you do but can't get paid? Hobby. Good at something the world doesn't need? Pointless. Paid well but hate it? Miserable. Ikigai is the sweet spot where all four align, creating sustainable purpose and satisfaction.

## How to Apply
Map the four circles:
- **What you love**: What energizes you? What would you do for free?
- **What you're good at**: What are your strengths and skills?
- **What the world needs**: What problems matter? What creates value?
- **What you can be paid for**: What do people pay for?

Find the overlap:
- Passion (love + good at)
- Mission (love + world needs)
- Vocation (world needs + paid for)
- Profession (good at + paid for)
- **Ikigai** (all four)

## Real-World Example
**Misaligned examples**:
- Lawyer who hates law: Paid well, good at it, world needs it, but doesn't love it = Empty success
- Talented artist who can't make money: Love it, good at it, but not paid and unclear if world needs it = Struggling passion
- Passionate teacher in oversaturated market: Love it, world needs it, but not well-paid or particularly skilled yet = Noble but unsustainable

**Aligned example - Ikigai**:
- Doctor who loves medicine, is skilled at surgery, helps patients in need, and earns a good living = All four circles overlap

**Finding your ikigai**:
Person loves writing, is good at explaining complex topics, world needs better science communication, and publications pay for quality science writing. Ikigai: Science writer/journalist.

**Career pivots**:
Software engineer (paid well, good at it) realizes they don't love it and the world is oversaturated with engineers. Discovers they love teaching and the world needs coding education. Becomes coding bootcamp instructor—closer to ikigai.

## Common Pitfalls
- Expecting perfect alignment immediately (it's a journey)
- Optimizing for only one or two circles (leads to dissatisfaction)
- Not reassessing as you grow (your ikigai can shift)
- Giving up one circle entirely (e.g., "do what you love and money will follow"—not always true)
- Analysis paralysis searching for perfect ikigai instead of taking steps toward it
