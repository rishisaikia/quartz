## Definition
Our models, descriptions, and representations of reality are not reality itself. The map (our mental model) is always a simplified, incomplete version of the territory (actual reality).

## Key Insight
We navigate life using mental maps—beliefs, theories, models, and assumptions about how things work. But these maps are always incomplete simplifications. Confusing your map with the territory leads to surprise, failure, and conflict. The best maps are useful, not accurate.

## How to Apply
Regularly remind yourself:
- "This is my model of reality, not reality itself"
- "What am I leaving out of this picture?"
- "Where might my map be outdated or wrong?"
- "Can I update my map with new information?"

Hold models lightly; update them freely.

## Real-World Example
A GPS map shows you the fastest route. You follow it blindly and end up at a closed road—construction that isn't on the map yet. The map was useful but incomplete. You need to navigate using the map AND observe the territory.

In business: You have a detailed spreadsheet projecting next quarter's revenue. The spreadsheet is precise, logical, and thorough—but it's a map. The actual territory includes competitor actions, market shifts, and customer behavior that aren't in your model. Treating the forecast as certain rather than provisional leads to poor decisions.

A doctor diagnoses based on symptoms and tests (the map). But sometimes the real disease (the territory) doesn't fit the textbook description. Good doctors know their diagnostic framework is a useful map, not absolute truth.

## Common Pitfalls
- Falling in love with your models and defending them over reality
- Thinking more detailed maps are always better (sometimes simple is better)
- Becoming a relativist who thinks all maps are equally (un)useful
