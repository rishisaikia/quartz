## Definition
People develop a preference for things simply because they're familiar with them. Repeated exposure to something increases liking, even without any positive experience or reward.

## Key Insight
Familiarity breeds comfort, not contempt. The more you encounter something, the more you tend to like it—even if you're not consciously aware of the exposures. This is why advertising works through repetition and why people prefer the familiar to the novel.

## How to Apply
Understanding mere exposure:
- Recognize that repeated exposure influences your preferences unconsciously
- Be aware that marketing exploits this (brand recognition = preference)
- Use it positively: Expose yourself repeatedly to things that are good for you
- In social situations: Simply being around people more makes them like you more

## Real-World Example
Songs you hated when you first heard them grow on you after hearing them repeatedly on the radio. You think you've developed appreciation for the music, but it's largely mere exposure effect—familiarity making it pleasant.

Brands spend millions on ads that don't explicitly persuade you of anything—just showing you their logo repeatedly. When you're at the store choosing between brands, you unconsciously prefer the familiar one. "I've heard of Coca-Cola" translates to "I prefer Coca-Cola."

In dating and friendship: The people you see regularly (coworkers, classmates, neighbors) become more attractive to you over time, not because they change but because familiarity creates preference.

Political campaigns know this: Getting the candidate's face on TV repeatedly, even without a message, increases voter preference. Name recognition is half the battle.

## Common Pitfalls
- Confusing familiarity with quality ("I prefer X" doesn't mean X is better)
- Being manipulated by advertising that relies solely on repetition
- Avoiding new experiences because the unfamiliar feels uncomfortable
- Not recognizing when your preferences are driven by exposure rather than genuine assessment
