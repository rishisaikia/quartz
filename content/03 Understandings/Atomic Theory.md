### Summary of Key Models:
1. **Democritus**: Atoms are indivisible and eternal.
2. **Dalton**: Atoms combine in fixed ratios; different elements have different atoms.
3. **Thomson**: Atoms contain electrons scattered in a positive "pudding."
4. **Rutherford**: Atoms have a dense, positively charged nucleus.
5. **Bohr**: Electrons orbit the nucleus in specific energy levels.
6. **Quantum Model**: Electrons exist in probabilistic orbitals, not fixed orbits.
7. **Chadwick**: Atoms have neutrons in the nucleus along with protons.

### 1. **Democritus’ Atomic Theory (~400 BCE)**:
   - **Proposed by**: Democritus, a Greek philosopher.
   - **Idea**: Matter is made up of small, indivisible particles called **"atomos"** (meaning "uncuttable"). He believed atoms were eternal and indestructible and that everything in the universe was composed of these particles.
   - **Limitations**: This was purely philosophical, without experimental proof.

### 2. **Dalton’s Atomic Theory (1803)**:
   - **Proposed by**: John Dalton, an English chemist.
   - **Idea**: 
     - All matter is made of tiny indivisible atoms.
     - Atoms of a given element are identical in mass and properties.
     - Atoms cannot be created, divided, or destroyed.
     - Atoms of different elements combine in fixed ratios to form compounds.
     - Chemical reactions involve the rearrangement of atoms.
   - **Importance**: Dalton’s theory introduced a scientific basis for atomic theory, using experimental evidence.
#### Key Postulates of Dalton's Atomic Theory

1. **All Matter is Composed of Atoms**: Dalton asserted that all matter consists of extremely small particles called atoms, which are indivisible and indestructible.

2. **Identical Atoms of an Element**: He claimed that all atoms of a given element are identical in size, mass, and properties. Conversely, atoms of different elements differ in size, mass, and properties.

3. **Atoms Cannot Be Created or Destroyed**: Dalton's theory stated that atoms cannot be created or destroyed in chemical reactions; they are merely rearranged.

4. **Whole Number Ratios**: Atoms of different elements can combine in simple whole-number ratios to form chemical compounds. This implies that compounds are formed from specific combinations of atoms.

5. **Rearrangement of Atoms**: In chemical reactions, atoms are combined, separated, or rearranged, but their total number remains constant.

#### Experimental Basis of Dalton's Theory

Dalton's atomic theory was informed by his meticulous experiments and observations regarding the behaviour of gases and the composition of compounds. He conducted experiments that focused on the weights of various elements and how they combined. Dalton noted that:

Elements combine in fixed ratios by mass, supporting the idea of definite proportions. He formulated the **Law of Multiple Proportions**, which states that when two elements form multiple compounds, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other can be expressed in small whole-number ratios. For example, if two different compounds are formed from the same elements, the mass ratios of the elements in these compounds will be simple whole numbers. In the case of carbon and oxygen:

>- 12 g of carbon combines with 16 g of oxygen to form CO
>- 12 g of carbon combines with 32 g of oxygen to form CO2

The ratio of oxygen masses is 32:16 = 2:1, a simple whole number ratio

Dalton's observations were foundational in establishing the atomic theory, even though some of his conclusions were later revised as scientific understanding evolved. His work laid the groundwork for modern chemistry by providing a systematic approach to understanding chemical reactions and the nature of matter.

### 3. **Thomson’s Plum Pudding Model (1904)**:
   - **Proposed by**: J.J. Thomson, an English physicist.
   - **Idea**: Atoms are made of a positively charged substance with negatively charged electrons scattered throughout, like plums in a pudding.
   - **Discovery**: Thomson discovered the electron, which showed that atoms were divisible.
   - **Limitations**: This model didn’t explain the structure of the nucleus or atomic stability.

This model was the first to suggest that atoms have an internal structure, consisting of negatively charged electrons embedded within a positively charged "soup," akin to plums within a pudding.
#### Key Features of the Plum Pudding Model

1. **Structure**: Thomson envisioned the atom as a uniform sphere of positive charge, with electrons scattered throughout. This arrangement was meant to ensure that the atom remained electrically neutral overall, as the negative charges of the electrons balanced the positive charge.

2. **Analogy**: The model is popularly referred to as the "plum pudding model" because it likens the electrons to plums (or raisins) distributed throughout a pudding (the positively charged sphere). However, Thomson himself did not use this analogy.

3. **Electrostatic Forces**: Thomson theorized that the electrostatic forces between the negatively charged electrons and the positively charged sphere kept the electrons in place, contributing to the stability of the atom.

#### Experimental Verification

J.J. Thomson employed several experimental methods to verify his plum pudding model of the atom, primarily through his work with cathode ray tubes. Here are the main methods he used:

##### Setup and Procedure

- **Cathode Ray Tube (CRT)**: Thomson constructed a glass tube from which most of the air had been evacuated, containing electrodes at either end (cathode and anode). When a high voltage was applied, cathode rays (streams of electrons) were emitted from the cathode.

##### Key Experimental Techniques

- **Magnetic Deflection**: Thomson used a magnet to observe the deflection of cathode rays. He noted that the rays were deflected in a consistent manner regardless of the material used for the cathode, suggesting that the rays were composed of the same type of particles (later identified as electrons) regardless of the source material. This indicated that electrons were a fundamental component of all atoms[3][4].

- **Electrical Deflection**: Thomson also applied an electric field to the cathode rays. By placing charged plates near the path of the rays, he observed that the rays bent towards the positively charged plate and away from the negatively charged plate. This confirmed that the rays were negatively charged, leading to the conclusion that they were composed of negatively charged particles (electrons) moving through a positively charged medium[3][6].

##### 2. Measurement of Charge-to-Mass Ratio

- **Determining e/m**: Thomson measured the charge-to-mass ratio of the cathode rays by manipulating electric and magnetic fields. He found that by adjusting these fields, he could balance the forces acting on the rays, allowing him to calculate their mass relative to their charge. This was a crucial step in establishing the properties of electrons[3][8].

##### 3. Mechanical Model Demonstration

- **Mechanical Analogy**: In 1905, Thomson demonstrated his model using a mechanical analogy involving magnetised pins floating in water. This experiment illustrated how negatively charged particles (electrons) could be arranged within a positively charged medium, similar to how the pins arranged themselves under the influence of magnetic forces. This helped visualise the stability of his proposed atomic structure[1][2].

Through these experimental methods, Thomson was able to provide substantial evidence for the existence of electrons and their arrangement within the atom, supporting his plum pudding model. However, this model was later challenged and ultimately replaced by Rutherford's nuclear model, which introduced a more accurate representation of atomic structure.

### 4. **Rutherford’s Nuclear Model (1911)**:
   - **Proposed by**: Ernest Rutherford, a New Zealand-born physicist.
   - **Idea**: 
     - Atoms have a small, dense, positively charged nucleus at the center, with electrons orbiting the nucleus, mostly in empty space.
     - The nucleus contains most of the atom’s mass.
   - **Experiment**: Rutherford’s gold foil experiment showed that some alpha particles were deflected, leading him to conclude that atoms have a dense core (the nucleus).
   - **Limitations**: It didn’t explain why electrons don’t spiral into the nucleus or how they stay in orbit.

Ernest Rutherford proposed the nuclear model of the atom in 1911 based on his famous gold foil experiment. The key features of Rutherford's model are:

- The positive charge and most of the mass of an atom is concentrated in a tiny, dense region called the **nucleus** at the center of the atom.

- The nucleus is surrounded by negatively charged **electrons** that orbit the nucleus in circular paths called **orbits**. 

- The electrons are held in these orbits by the electrostatic force of attraction between the positive charge of the nucleus and the negative charge of the electrons.

- Most of the volume of an atom is empty space. The nucleus occupies only a very small fraction of the total volume.

##### Rutherford's Gold Foil Experiment

To arrive at this model, Rutherford directed an experiment where a beam of positively charged alpha particles was fired at a thin sheet of gold foil:

1. **Setup**: A radioactive source emitted a beam of alpha particles which were collimated into a narrow beam by passing through a slit.

2. **Observation**: Most of the alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil with no deflection. A small number were deflected at small angles. 

3. **Surprising Result**: A very small fraction of the alpha particles (about 1 in 8000) were deflected at very large angles, with some even bouncing straight back at the source.

##### Rutherford's Conclusions

- The atom must contain a tiny, dense, positively charged region (the nucleus) to cause the large angle deflections

- Most of the atom is empty space, allowing the majority of alpha particles to pass through undeflected

- The electrons orbit in the empty space around the nucleus

This led Rutherford to propose his nuclear model of the atom, which was a major advance over Thomson's "plum pudding" model. The gold foil experiment provided the first evidence of the atomic nucleus and the mostly empty space within atoms.

### 5. **Bohr’s Planetary Model (1913)**:
   - **Proposed by**: Niels Bohr, a Danish physicist.
   - **Idea**: 
     - Electrons orbit the nucleus in specific energy levels (quantised orbits).
     - Electrons can jump between energy levels, absorbing or emitting energy in the form of light.
     - Electrons closer to the nucleus have lower energy, and those farther away have higher energy.
   - **Importance**: Bohr’s model explained the emission spectra of hydrogen but was limited to hydrogen-like atoms.

Niels Bohr developed the **planetary model** of the atom in 1913, which was a significant advancement over previous atomic models, especially Rutherford's nuclear model. Bohr's model introduced the concept of quantized energy levels for electrons, fundamentally altering the understanding of atomic structure.

#### Key Features of Bohr's Planetary Model

1. **Electrons in Fixed Orbits**: Bohr proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus in specific, stable circular paths called **orbits** or **shells**. Unlike previous models, where electrons could occupy any position, Bohr's model restricted electrons to certain allowed orbits.

2. **Quantised Energy Levels**: Each orbit corresponds to a specific energy level. Electrons can only exist in these discrete energy states and cannot exist between them. The energy of an electron is lower in orbits closer to the nucleus and higher in orbits further away.

3. **Energy Absorption and Emission**: When an electron moves from a higher energy level to a lower one, it emits energy in the form of light (photons). Conversely, to move to a higher energy level, an electron must absorb energy. The energy of the emitted or absorbed photon corresponds to the difference in energy between the two levels.

4. **Hydrogen Spectrum Explanation**: Bohr's model successfully explained the **line spectrum** of hydrogen, which had been observed experimentally but not understood. The distinct lines in the spectrum correspond to the specific energy transitions of electrons between quantised orbits.

##### Experimental Basis for Bohr's Model

Bohr's model was influenced by several key experiments and theoretical insights:

1. **Rydberg Formula**: Before Bohr, the Rydberg formula described the wavelengths of spectral lines in hydrogen but lacked a theoretical foundation. Bohr's model provided this foundation by linking the observed spectral lines to quantized energy levels.

2. **Franck-Hertz Experiment (1914)**: Although conducted after Bohr's initial model, the Franck-Hertz experiment provided direct evidence for quantized energy levels in atoms. In this experiment, electrons were accelerated and collided with mercury atoms, resulting in energy emissions that confirmed the existence of discrete energy states, supporting Bohr's theory.

3. **Planck's Quantum Theory**: Bohr incorporated Max Planck's concept of quantization of energy, which stated that energy is absorbed or emitted in discrete units (quanta). This idea was crucial for understanding how electrons could occupy specific energy levels without spiraling into the nucleus, as classical physics would suggest.

By integrating these theoretical insights and experimental results, Bohr was able to propose a model that not only explained the hydrogen spectrum but also laid the groundwork for modern quantum mechanics. Although later developments in quantum theory would refine and expand upon Bohr's model, it remains a pivotal moment in the history of atomic physics.

### 6. **Quantum Mechanical Model (1926)**:
   - **Developed by**: Erwin Schrödinger, Werner Heisenberg, and others.
   - **Idea**:
     - Electrons do not travel in fixed orbits; instead, they exist in regions called **orbitals**, where there is a high probability of finding an electron.
     - This model uses wave mechanics and probability to describe electron behavior.
     - The location and energy of electrons can be described using a mathematical function called the **wavefunction**.
     - Heisenberg’s **uncertainty principle** suggests we cannot know both the exact position and momentum of an electron simultaneously.
   - **Importance**: This is the most accurate and accepted model today, based on quantum mechanics.

### 7. **Chadwick’s Discovery of the Neutron (1932)**:
   - **Proposed by**: James Chadwick, an English physicist.
   - **Idea**: The nucleus contains not only protons but also neutral particles called **neutrons**, which contribute to the mass of the atom but have no charge.
   - **Importance**: This discovery completed the modern understanding of atomic structure, explaining the mass of atoms and the existence of isotopes.
