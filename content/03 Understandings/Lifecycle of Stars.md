Stars are born, live, and die in a fascinating cycle that can span millions to billions of years. Their paths depend significantly on their mass. Here’s a detailed look at each stage:

### 1. **Nebula**

- **Definition**: A nebula is a vast cloud of gas (mostly hydrogen) and dust in space. It is often the birthplace of stars.
- **Example**: The Orion Nebula is one of the most famous star-forming regions visible from Earth.

### 2. **Protostar**

- **Formation**: As gravity pulls the gas and dust together, it forms a dense core called a protostar. This core heats up as it contracts.
- **Characteristics**: Protostars are not yet hot enough to start nuclear fusion but are surrounded by a rotating disk of material.
- **Duration**: This stage lasts about 100,000 years.

### 3. **Main Sequence Star**

- **Definition**: Once the core temperature reaches about 10 million degrees Celsius, hydrogen fusion begins, marking the star's entry into the main sequence phase.
- **Characteristics**: Stars spend about 90% of their lives in this stable phase, balancing the gravitational force pulling inward and the outward pressure from nuclear fusion.
- **Examples**:
  - **The Sun**: A G-type main-sequence star (G dwarf) that has been shining for about 4.6 billion years.
  - **Sirius**: The brightest star in the night sky, a binary system with a main-sequence star (Sirius A) that is about twice as massive as the Sun.

### 4. **Red Giant Phase**

In main sequence, star has hydrostatic equilibrium, where the gravitational force on the mass of the star is counterbalanced by the energy produced by hydrogen fusion. When a star exhausts its hydrogen fuel, several key processes trigger its expansion into a red giant:

1. **Core Contraction**: As hydrogen in the core runs out, fusion stops, reducing the outward pressure that counteracts gravity. The core then contracts under its own weight, increasing temperature and pressure[1][5].

2. **Helium Fusion Initiation**: The rising temperature eventually allows helium fusion to begin in the core (the triple-alpha process). In some stars, this can occur suddenly in a dramatic event known as a **helium flash**[2][6].

3. **Hydrogen Shell Fusion**: Meanwhile, hydrogen fusion starts in a shell surrounding the core. This shell burning generates significant energy, which heats the outer layers of the star[4][5].

4. **Expansion**: The energy produced from both helium and hydrogen shell fusion causes the outer layers to expand significantly, transforming the star into a red giant or supergiant[3][4]. 

This sequence of events fundamentally alters the star's structure and luminosity, leading to its dramatic growth.

- **Transition**: After exhausting hydrogen in its core, the star begins to fuse helium into heavier elements like carbon and oxygen.
- **Expansion**: The outer layers expand significantly, and the star cools, giving it a reddish appearance.
- **Duration**: This phase can last a few hundred million years.
- **Examples**:
  - **Betelgeuse**: A well-known red supergiant in the constellation Orion, nearing the end of its life cycle.
  - **Antares**: Another red supergiant in Scorpius, often referred to as "the heart of the scorpion."

### 5. **Death of Low-Mass Stars**

Low-mass stars (up to about 8 times the mass of the Sun) follow this path:

#### A. **Planetary Nebula**

- **Process**: The outer layers are expelled into space, creating a beautiful shell of gas known as a planetary nebula.
- **Example**: The Ring Nebula (M57) is a classic example of this stage.

#### B. **White Dwarf**

- **Final Stage**: The core left behind becomes a white dwarf, which is hot but no longer undergoing fusion. Over billions of years, it cools and dims.
- **Example**: Sirius B is a white dwarf that was once part of a binary system with Sirius A.

### 6. **Death of High-Mass Stars**

High-mass stars (more than about 8 times the mass of the Sun) have a more dramatic end:

#### A. **Supernova**

- **Explosion**: When iron builds up in the core and fusion can no longer occur, the core collapses under gravity, leading to an explosive release of energy—a supernova.
- **Example**: SN 1987A was a supernova observed in 1987 in the Large Magellanic Cloud.

#### B. **Neutron Star or Black Hole**

- Depending on the remaining mass after the explosion:
    - If it’s between about 1.4 to 3 times solar mass, it becomes a neutron star (incredibly dense).
    - If it's more than three solar masses, it collapses into a black hole.
- **Examples**:
    - **Pulsar PSR B1919+21** is a famous neutron star known for its regular pulses of radiation.
    - **Cygnus X-1**, one of the first black holes discovered, is part of a binary system.

## Summary

The lifecycle of stars is an incredible journey from nebulous beginnings to explosive endings or quiet fades into white dwarfs. Each type of star has its unique path influenced by its initial mass and composition.
