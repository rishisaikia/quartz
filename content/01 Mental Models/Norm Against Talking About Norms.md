## Definition
There's often a meta-level prohibition against explicitly discussing certain social norms. We have unspoken rules against naming unspoken rules, because explicitly acknowledging them would undermine their function.

## Key Insight
Some social arrangements only work when they remain implicit. Making them explicit—turning private knowledge into [[Common Knowledge]]—forces people to consciously respond to them, which changes everything. We maintain a norm against discussing certain norms because naming them would destroy them.

## How to Apply
Recognize the meta-norm:
- "Why does discussing this feel taboo?"
- "What would change if we named this pattern openly?"
- "Why are people uncomfortable when I point this out?"

Navigate it carefully:
- Some norms work better unstated
- Naming them can make you seem cynical or socially inappropriate
- But sometimes making norms explicit is necessary for change

## Real-World Example
**Education as signaling:**
Everyone somewhat knows education is partly about credentials and signaling. But we don't openly say "college is mostly a four-year IQ test for employers." Teachers present it as learning, students pretend to be there to learn, employers pretend degrees prove knowledge. Making it explicit would undermine the whole system.

**Gift-giving:**
Gifts are partly about signaling affection, partly about status display, partly reciprocity obligations. But saying "I'm giving you this to signal my affection and create reciprocity obligations" would ruin it. The gift must be presented as pure generosity. The norm against discussing the strategic elements preserves the system.

**Dating and attraction:**
People know physical attractiveness matters enormously in dating. But openly saying "I'm dating you because you're hot, which signals good genes and status to others" is taboo. We frame it as personality, connection, and shared values. Naming the superficial motives would be relationship-destroying.

**Office politics:**
Everyone knows promotions involve politics, alliances, and visibility management. But overtly discussing "I'm befriending the VP to advance my career" violates norms. We frame it as genuine friendship and professional development. Making it explicit would be seen as manipulative.

**Pointing out the meta-norm:**
Notice what happens when someone says "everyone here is just virtue signaling" or "this is all about status." Even if true, they're violating the norm against naming norms. People respond with discomfort or hostility—not because they disagree, but because you're breaking the meta-rule.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming you're revealing hidden truths when you name norms (people already know, they just don't discuss it)
- Not recognizing why the meta-norm exists (some things work better unstated)
- Being seen as cynical by constantly naming unspoken motives
- Missing that you're following the same meta-norms you're critiquing
- Thinking all norms should be made explicit (some genuinely work better implicit)

## Notes
In [[The Elephant in the Brain]], Robin Hanson and Kevin Simler argue that the norm against talking about norms is functional, not arbitrary. Many social institutions depend on maintaining what they call "strategic ignorance" about true motives.

**Why the Meta-Norm Exists:**
Making hidden motives explicit converts them into [[Common Knowledge]], which forces social responses. As long as motives remain officially unacknowledged, people can pursue them while maintaining plausible deniability. Once explicitly stated, they must be defended or condemned.

For example: Everyone knows some charity is about status signaling. But as long as this isn't openly acknowledged, people can claim altruistic motives and receive social credit. If we openly said "charity is status signaling," people would have to either defend status-seeking (socially costly) or stop donating (reducing total charity). The implicit understanding allows the system to function.

**The Book's Central Argument:**
Hanson and Simler's entire thesis depends on this meta-norm. They argue that education is largely signaling, healthcare is partly about showing care rather than health outcomes, and political beliefs are more about tribal affiliation than policy outcomes. But we can't openly acknowledge these hidden motives because doing so would undermine the institutions.

We say we go to school "to learn," donate "to help others," vote "for good policy"—and we genuinely believe these. But we also serve hidden motives (signaling, status, tribalism) that can't be openly acknowledged without social cost.

**The Violation Penalty:**
The book notes that people who point out hidden motives are often seen as cynical, unsophisticated, or socially inappropriate—even when they're correct. This isn't because they're wrong; it's because they're violating the meta-norm. They're making [[Common Knowledge]] what should remain [[Pluralistic Ignorance]].

Academic tenure exists partly because scholars need protection to violate this meta-norm. Hanson and Simler can write this book partly because they have tenure. Without institutional protection, explicitly naming hidden motives carries significant social costs.

**When Violation Is Necessary:**
The authors acknowledge that sometimes we must violate the meta-norm to enable change. If a social arrangement is genuinely harmful, maintaining polite silence perpetuates harm. Whistleblowers, activists, and reformers necessarily violate the norm against naming norms.

But they emphasize the cost: you'll be seen as difficult, cynical, or naive (for thinking others don't already know). The resistance you face isn't because people don't know what you're saying—it's because you're threatening an implicit arrangement that serves multiple parties.

**Self-Awareness Without Cynicism:**
The book's final chapters grapple with this tension: How do you become aware of hidden motives without becoming cynical? How do you acknowledge that education is signaling while still valuing learning? Their answer: Both motives are real. The hidden motives don't negate the stated ones—they coexist. Understanding this allows sophisticated navigation of social reality without either naive idealism or destructive cynicism.
