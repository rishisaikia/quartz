## Definition
When the output of a system circles back to become input, either amplifying (positive feedback) or dampening (negative feedback) the original action. Self-reinforcing or self-correcting cycles.

## Key Insight
Most important changes aren't linear cause-and-effect—they're circular. Your actions create results that change the context for your next actions. Understanding these loops reveals leverage points for intervention and explains why small changes can have big effects (or why big efforts sometimes fail).

## How to Apply
Map the loops in any system:
- What feeds back into what?
- Are loops amplifying (positive) or balancing (negative)?
- Where could you intervene to shift the whole system?
- What delayed effects might create surprising loops?

## Real-World Example
Positive (amplifying) loop: A company builds a great product → attracts talented employees → builds better products → attracts more talent. Success breeds success until something breaks the cycle.

Negative (balancing) loop: You eat too much sugar → blood sugar spikes → body releases insulin → blood sugar drops → you crave sugar again. Your body tries to maintain balance.

Combined loops: Social media posts with engagement get shown to more people (positive loop amplifying popular content) but if users report them, they get suppressed (negative loop maintaining quality). The system's behavior emerges from how these loops interact.

A company cuts prices to increase sales. Sales increase temporarily, but lower prices mean less revenue to invest in quality, so product quality drops, so customers leave, forcing more price cuts. A downward spiral created by positive feedback.

## Common Pitfalls
- Only seeing the first-order effect, missing the feedback
- Intervening without understanding which loops you're affecting
- Assuming positive feedback loops continue indefinitely (they don't)
