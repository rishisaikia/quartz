## Definition
Information that everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows, and everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone knows (and so on). It's not enough for something to be true and widely known—it must be publicly known to be known.

## Key Insight
*Private knowledge and common knowledge lead to completely different behaviors*. Everyone might privately believe something, but until it becomes common knowledge (publicly acknowledged), people won't act on it. This is why public statements, announcements, and symbolic actions matter so much—they convert private knowledge into common knowledge.

## How to Apply
Understand the difference:
- **Mutual knowledge**: We all know X
- **Common knowledge**: We all know X, and we all know that we all know X, and we all know that we all know that we all know X...

Ask: "Does everyone know? Or does everyone know that everyone knows?"

Creating change often requires making private beliefs public.

## Real-World Example
The Emperor's New Clothes: Everyone privately knows the emperor is naked. But no one will say it because they don't know that others know. When the child shouts "He's naked!", it becomes common knowledge—everyone now knows that everyone else knows. Suddenly everyone feels safe acknowledging the truth.

In a company, everyone might privately think a project is failing, but until someone says it in a meeting (making it common knowledge), people keep working on it. The moment a VP says "This isn't working," it becomes safe for others to agree.

Financial bubbles: Many investors privately suspect prices are inflated, but they keep buying because they don't know others share their suspicion. When a major investor publicly says "This is a bubble," it can trigger a crash—common knowledge enables coordinated action (selling).

Protests and revolutions: Citizens might all want change but fear being the only one. A public demonstration makes opposition common knowledge—"I know others oppose this, they know I oppose it, and we all know we're not alone." This enables coordination and collective action.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming everyone acts on what "everyone knows" (they don't—it must be common knowledge)
- Underestimating the power of public statements versus private beliefs
- Not recognizing when you need to make something common knowledge to enable action
- Confusing "everyone believes X" with "it's common knowledge that everyone believes X"

## Notes
In their book [[The Elephant in the Brain]], Robin Hanson and Kevin Simler argue that many social institutions depend on maintaining ambiguity about our true motives. While everyone may privately suspect the real reasons behind behaviors, these arrangements function precisely because such motives don't become common knowledge.

When something becomes common knowledge, you can no longer maintain plausible deniability. Everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows, and therefore everyone must now _socially respond_ to this knowledge. This changes everything:

**1. Social Cooperation Becomes Harder**
Many social arrangements depend on polite fictions. For example: Your boss says the unpaid internship is a "learning opportunity." You know it's cheap labor. Your boss knows you know. But as long as this isn't common knowledge, you can both proceed with the arrangement. Once it becomes common knowledge that it's exploitation, your boss can't maintain the pretense, you can't pretend to be grateful, and the relationship becomes explicitly transactional and uncomfortable.

**2. Reputational Costs Become Unavoidable**
If everyone _privately_ suspects you're donating to charity primarily for status, you can still claim altruistic motives. But once it's common knowledge that you're doing it for status, you can't credibly claim to be altruistic anymore. The status benefit actually decreases because everyone knows you're just doing it for status.

This creates a paradox: many status-seeking behaviors only work if the status-seeking motive remains hidden. Making it common knowledge destroys the very benefit you're seeking.

**3. You Lose Strategic Flexibility**
As long as motives remain ambiguous:

- A politician can appeal to different constituencies with different interpretations of the same policy
- A company can claim "restructuring is about innovation" while everyone understands it's cost-cutting
- You can maintain relationships with people whose values differ from yours

Once motives become common knowledge, you're forced to pick sides explicitly, defend positions, or admit things you'd rather not admit.

**4. Moral Accountability Gets Triggered**
Private knowledge allows moral wiggle room. Common knowledge triggers social enforcement:

- If it's common knowledge that your expensive healthcare is more about signaling care than effective treatment, someone might challenge: "Why are you wasting resources?"
- If it's common knowledge that education is mostly signaling, someone asks: "Why are we forcing everyone through this costly charade?"
- If everyone openly acknowledges politics is tribal performance, the whole system loses legitimacy

**5. The Social Game Changes**
Think about a job interview. You know you're selling yourself. The interviewer knows you're selling yourself. But the game requires you to act like you're having an authentic conversation about mutual fit. If the interviewer said, "We both know you're just telling me what I want to hear," the game collapses. You can't play anymore.

**Why Common Knowledge Is Different from Private Knowledge**
The key insight: when everyone _privately_ knows something, you can still act as if it's not true. Social norms, etiquette, and coordination can proceed based on the official story. But when something is _common_ knowledge:

- You can be called out for pretending otherwise
- Social consequences become unavoidable
- The emperor-has-no-clothes moment forces everyone to respond

**Conclusion**
Many of our social institutions and relationships _require_ a certain level of collective self-deception to function. They work precisely because we maintain ambiguity about motives. Common knowledge destroys this ambiguity and forces everyone to deal with uncomfortable truths explicitly.

This is why we're so motivated to keep certain things from becoming common knowledge—not just to protect our own image, but because making them explicit would break the social games we all benefit from playing.

The book suggests we're not just hiding motives from others, but from ourselves as well, precisely because genuine self-deception makes us more effective at preventing our true motives from becoming common knowledge.
