## Definition
The tendency to prefer things to stay the same, even when change would be beneficial. We default to the current state and require strong evidence before making changes.

## Key Insight
The status quo has an unfair advantage in our decision-making. We treat keeping things the same as "safe" or "neutral" when it's actually a choice with consequences. This bias makes us stick with bad jobs, bad relationships, and bad investments far too long.

## How to Apply
Combat status quo bias by:
- Asking "If I were starting fresh today, would I choose this?"
- Treating the current state as one option among many
- Deliberately seeking reasons to change, not just reasons to stay
- Setting regular "review points" to reconsider defaults

## Real-World Example
Your bank offers 0.1% interest on savings while another bank offers 2%. You know you should switch but it feels like effort, so you do nothing. You're choosing to lose money every year because switching feels harder than staying put, even though switching takes 20 minutes.

Companies keep using outdated software because "that's what we've always used," even when better options exist. The switching cost feels real; the daily inefficiency cost feels invisible.

You stay at a job you dislike because it's familiar. Starting a job search feels overwhelming. You rationalize: "It's not that bad." But you're choosing to be unhappy every day because change feels risky.

Most people stick with default retirement plan allocations, default organ donor status, default privacy settings—whatever was pre-selected—even when changing would clearly benefit them.

## Common Pitfalls
- Overcorrecting and changing everything constantly
- Not distinguishing between status quo bias and genuine stability
- Underestimating real switching costs in pursuit of optimization
