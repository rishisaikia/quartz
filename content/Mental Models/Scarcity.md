## Definition
People value things more when they're rare or in limited supply. Scarcity (real or perceived) increases perceived value and urgency to act.

## Key Insight
We don't just want things because they're valuable—we want them MORE when we think we might lose access to them. Scarcity triggers loss aversion and competitive instincts. Marketers exploit this, but it's also a genuine signal of value in many contexts.

## How to Apply
Recognize scarcity tactics:
- "Only 3 left in stock" — Is this real or manufactured?
- "Sale ends tonight" — Is urgency justified?
- "Limited edition" — Does rarity actually add value?

Use scarcity ethically:
- Set real deadlines for decisions
- Be honest about limited capacity
- Value your own time (it truly is scarce)

## Real-World Example
A concert announces "Final 100 tickets!" and they sell out in hours. The exact same concert with "Plenty of tickets available" sells slowly. The music is identical; the scarcity creates urgency.

Supreme drops limited quantities of clothing. People camp overnight to buy $50 t-shirts they could make for $5 because scarcity creates social value. The scarcity IS the product.

A consultant who says "I only take 3 clients per quarter" gets paid more and respected more than one who says "I have lots of availability." Scarcity of access signals high value (whether deserved or not).

Job postings with "accepting applications through Friday only" get more and faster responses than "rolling applications." The same deadline creates urgency even though the job is the same.

## Common Pitfalls
- Making impulsive decisions because of artificial scarcity
- Creating fake scarcity that damages trust when discovered
- Confusing scarcity with value (rare doesn't always mean good)
- Missing genuine opportunities because you assume all scarcity is manufactured
