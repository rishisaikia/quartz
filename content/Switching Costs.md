## Definition
The costs (time, money, effort, learning, risk) associated with changing from one option to another. High switching costs keep people locked into suboptimal choices.

## Key Insight
People don't always choose the best option—they choose the best option minus the switching cost. A competitor can be 10% better but still lose if switching costs are 15%. Understanding switching costs explains customer loyalty, market dominance, and why change is hard.

## How to Apply
When selling or competing:
- Reduce switching costs for your customers (make it easy to try)
- Increase switching costs for competitors' customers (create lock-in)
- Highlight total cost including switching, not just price

When buying or choosing:
- Account for switching costs realistically
- Sometimes paying high switching costs now prevents worse lock-in later
- Low switching costs = more flexibility = valuable

## Real-World Example
**Banking**: Your bank has mediocre rates and poor service. Better banks exist. Why don't you switch? Switching costs: updating all autopay bills, direct deposits, linked accounts, learning new app, potential gaps in service. The hassle keeps you with a suboptimal bank even though switching would take just a few hours.

**Software**: A company uses outdated project management software. Better tools exist, but switching means: migrating data, retraining employees, changing workflows, risking data loss, paying for overlap during transition. The switching cost ($50k-$100k in time and risk) keeps them on inferior software saving $20k/year.

**Relationships**: Unhappy relationships persist partly due to switching costs: shared lease, mutual friends, established routines, fear of dating again, splitting possessions. These costs keep people in relationships they'd never enter if starting fresh.

**Cloud providers**: Once you build on AWS, switching to Google Cloud is expensive—rewrite code, retrain engineers, migration risks. AWS knows this and uses it. The switching cost creates customer lock-in worth billions.

**Habits and routines**: Switching from driving to biking has switching costs: buying bike, learning routes, being slower initially, sweating at work. Even if biking is better long-term, the switching cost prevents change.

## Common Pitfalls
- Underestimating switching costs when evaluating alternatives
- Staying with bad options because you overestimate switching costs
- Not recognizing when companies deliberately create switching costs
- Ignoring opportunity cost of NOT switching despite switching costs
