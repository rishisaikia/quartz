## Definition
Unwritten rules that govern behavior in groups and societies. Standards of acceptable conduct that people follow not because of laws, but because of social expectations and consequences.

## Key Insight
Most of our behavior is governed by invisible norms we barely notice. We follow them automatically and feel uncomfortable when others break them. Norms are incredibly powerful at shaping behavior, but they're also arbitrary and can change rapidly once enough people stop following them.

## How to Apply
Understanding norms:
- Notice the unwritten rules in different contexts
- Ask: "What would happen if I broke this norm?"
- Recognize that norms vary by culture, context, and group
- Change requires either authority figures breaking the norm first, or enough people breaking it simultaneously

Use norms strategically:
- To change behavior, change the perceived norm
- To fit in, follow norms; to innovate, selectively break them

## Real-World Example
Personal space: In most Western cultures, standing very close to a stranger feels violating, even though there's no law against it. The discomfort you feel is norm enforcement. In other cultures, the norm for personal space is completely different.

Tipping in restaurants: In the US, not tipping 15-20% feels wrong and you face social judgment. In Japan, tipping can be seen as insulting. Same action, opposite norms.

Smoking: In the 1950s, the norm was that smoking was sophisticated and acceptable everywhere. Social pressure made people start smoking. Today, the norm flipped—smoking is seen negatively, and social pressure makes people quit. No one passed a law saying "thou shalt not think smoking is cool," but the norm changed completely.

Wearing masks during COVID: Initially weird and suspicious, then became the norm (in many places), then shifted back. Norms can change rapidly when conditions change and enough people start behaving differently.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming norms are natural or inevitable (they're socially constructed)
- Mistaking local norms for universal truths
- Following harmful norms just because "that's how things are done"
- Not recognizing that you have power to shift norms by behaving differently
- Underestimating how quickly norms can change once they start shifting
