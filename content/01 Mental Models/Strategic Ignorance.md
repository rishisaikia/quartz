## Definition
Sometimes it's advantageous not to know things. Deliberately avoiding information or maintaining uncertainty protects you from obligations, accountability, or uncomfortable truths.

## Key Insight
Information creates obligations. Knowing something means you must act on it or explain why you didn't. Strategic ignorance preserves flexibility and plausible deniability. This isn't just about deceiving others—we strategically keep ourselves ignorant to avoid internal conflict and maintain our preferred narratives.

## How to Apply
Recognize strategic ignorance:
- "Why don't I want to know this?"
- "What would knowing force me to do?"
- "Am I avoiding information to maintain deniability?"

Use it carefully:
- Some ignorance is genuinely protective
- But it can enable harmful complicity
- Balance self-protection with ethical responsibility

## Real-World Example
**Corporate leadership:**
A CEO doesn't want to know about questionable practices in their company. If they don't know, they can't be held responsible. They might vaguely suspect problems but avoid asking direct questions—maintaining plausible deniability. "I had no idea this was happening" only works if you genuinely didn't investigate.

**Personal finance:**
You avoid checking your bank account when you know you've been overspending. Knowing the exact number would force you to make painful changes. Not knowing lets you continue spending while maintaining "I didn't realize it was that bad."

**Relationship issues:**
You don't ask your partner direct questions about concerning behavior because you don't want to know the answer. Confirming your suspicions would force you to address it or leave. Ignorance preserves the relationship (temporarily) while knowing would destroy it.

**Donations and consumption:**
You don't research factory farming conditions or sweatshop labor because knowing the details would create guilt about your consumption. Vague awareness lets you continue as before; detailed knowledge would obligate change.

**Medical testing:**
Someone avoids genetic testing for diseases they can't prevent. Knowing they have the gene would cause anxiety without enabling action. Ignorance preserves peace of mind.

## Common Pitfalls
- Using strategic ignorance to avoid ethical responsibility
- Not recognizing when you're keeping yourself ignorant
- Assuming all ignorance is strategic (some is genuine)
- Maintaining ignorance when the cost becomes too high
- Judging others for strategic ignorance while practicing it yourself

## Notes
In [[The Elephant in the Brain]], Robin Hanson and Kevin Simler argue that strategic ignorance isn't a personal failing—it's a rational response to social reality. We maintain ignorance about our own motives and external facts because knowledge would create costs we prefer to avoid.

**Self-Ignorance About Motives:**
The book's central claim is that we're strategically ignorant about our own hidden motives. Our [[Press Secretary Brain]] doesn't have full access to why we do things because knowing would make us worse liars. If you consciously knew you were pursuing status through charity, your face would betray you. By genuinely not knowing (or not fully acknowledging) your selfish motives, you can pursue them more effectively.

This is why confronting someone with their hidden motives often fails—they genuinely don't consciously know. The ignorance is strategic but not conscious. Your brain keeps certain information from your conscious awareness precisely because conscious knowledge would be disadvantageous.

**Institutional Plausible Deniability:**
Organizations use strategic ignorance systematically. Hanson and Simler give examples:
- Managers don't want to know if subordinates are cutting corners
- Politicians avoid details about morally questionable policies
- Institutions maintain separation between departments to preserve deniability
- Companies don't investigate supplier practices too closely

This isn't always malicious. Sometimes strategic ignorance is protective. A lawyer who doesn't ask if their client is guilty can defend them more zealously. A journalist who doesn't reveal sources can protect them. Some ignorance enables cooperation.

**The Commons Knowledge Connection:**
Strategic ignorance prevents information from becoming [[Common Knowledge]]. As long as something isn't officially known, it doesn't require official response. Once it becomes common knowledge (everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows), institutional response is mandatory.

Example: Everyone might privately suspect a colleague is underperforming, but as long as this isn't officially documented, management can avoid firing them. Once it becomes common knowledge through formal complaints, management must act. Strategic ignorance preserves flexibility.

**The Cost of Knowledge:**
The book explains why we resist learning uncomfortable truths about ourselves and our institutions. Knowledge creates obligations:
- Knowing you're harming others obligates change or accepting guilt
- Knowing institutions you support are corrupt forces you to withdraw support or become complicit
- Knowing your beliefs are wrong requires updating or defending the indefensible

Strategic ignorance avoids these dilemmas. You can continue supporting institutions, maintaining beliefs, and pursuing goals without the psychological cost of conscious hypocrisy.

**When Strategic Ignorance Fails:**
Hanson and Simler acknowledge the dark side. Strategic ignorance enables terrible things—from individual bad behavior to institutional corruption. At some point, the cost of ignorance exceeds the benefit, and we must choose between knowledge and complicity.

The book doesn't prescribe when to seek knowledge versus maintain ignorance. It simply reveals that we all practice strategic ignorance more than we admit, and that this isn't always irrational. Understanding the pattern helps us recognize when we're using it and decide consciously whether to maintain it or confront reality.
