## Definition
In a hierarchy, people are promoted based on performance in their current role until they reach a position where they're incompetent. Everyone rises to their level of incompetence.

## Key Insight
Good performance in one job doesn't guarantee good performance in the next level up. Being a great engineer doesn't make you a great manager. But organizations promote based on current performance, ensuring that eventually everyone ends up in a role they're bad at—and stays there (because they're not performing well enough to be promoted further).

## How to Apply
Avoid the Peter Principle:
- Recognize that promotion isn't always the answer
- Skills for one level ≠ skills for next level
- Consider lateral moves to roles that fit your strengths
- Train for the next role before promoting
- Allow "individual contributor" tracks for specialists

Spot it in organizations:
- "Why is everyone mediocre at their jobs?"
- "Why did we promote our best X into a management role they're terrible at?"

## Real-World Example
**Tech companies**: Brilliant programmer gets promoted to team lead because they write great code. But team lead requires communication, delegation, and people management—completely different skills. They struggle, team suffers, but they don't get demoted (that's rare). They've reached their level of incompetence.

**Sales organizations**: Top salesperson gets promoted to sales manager. Being good at selling and being good at managing salespeople are different skills. The company loses a great salesperson and gains a mediocre manager. Peter Principle in action.

**Academia**: Excellent researcher becomes department chair. Research requires deep focus and individual work; department chair requires politics, administration, and people management. They're miserable and bad at it, but they've been "promoted."

**Military**: Great battlefield tactician promoted to strategic command. Tactics and strategy require different thinking. They struggle in the new role but can't go back to their old level.

**Your career**: You were great at your job, got promoted, and now you're overwhelmed and performing poorly. You've hit your level of incompetence. The solution isn't to work harder—it's to either develop new skills or move to a role that fits your strengths.

## Common Pitfalls
- Accepting every promotion without considering if you'll be good at the new role
- Promoting people as a reward rather than based on fit for the new role
- Not creating alternative advancement paths for specialists
- Shame around admitting a promotion isn't working
- Organizations failing to train people for new roles before promoting them
