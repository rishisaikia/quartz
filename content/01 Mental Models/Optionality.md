## Definition
Creating situations where you have the right, but not the obligation, to take advantage of favorable outcomes. Preserving future choices and opportunities.

## Key Insight
The most robust strategies keep options open rather than committing to a single path. Options have asymmetric payoffs: limited downside, unlimited upside. The value isn't in the choice you make, but in having multiple choices to make.

## How to Apply
Design situations where:
- You can benefit from positive events without being hurt by negative ones
- You maintain flexibility to adapt to new information
- You invest small amounts to create large potential payoffs
- You can walk away if things don't work out

Ask: "What would preserve my ability to choose later?"

## Real-World Example
You're deciding between two job offers: one with a huge salary at a declining company, another with a smaller salary at a growing startup with stock options. The startup job has optionality—if the company fails, you lose some salary but gain experience. If it succeeds, you could make millions from the stock. The high-salary job has less optionality—it's just the salary.

A student choosing a major: Engineering provides more optionality than something hyper-specialized. An engineering degree can lead to engineering, consulting, finance, startups, teaching, or many other paths. A narrow specialization commits you to fewer future paths.

Dating multiple people (ethically/transparently) before committing maintains optionality. Getting engaged to the first person you date eliminates options.

## Common Pitfalls
- Hoarding options and never committing to anything
- Paying too much to maintain unnecessary options
- Confusing optionality with indecisiveness
