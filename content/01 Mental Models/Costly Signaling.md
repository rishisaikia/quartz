## Definition
A signal is credible to the degree it is costly to produce. If a signal is cheap, anyone can fake it. If a signal is genuinely expensive—in time, money, risk, or sacrifice—then only those who truly possess the underlying quality can afford to send it.

## Key Insight
The wastefulness is the point. A peacock's tail wastes energy and increases predation risk. That's precisely what makes it credible: only a genuinely fit bird survives despite the handicap. Whenever you see apparently wasteful behavior in competitive contexts, look for what it signals.

## How to Apply
Evaluating signals:
- Ask: "What would this cost a faker?"
- The higher the cost, the more credible the signal
- Zero-cost signals (promises, claims) should be discounted heavily

Designing signals:
- If you need to be believed, find signals imposters can't afford
- Be wary of cheaper substitutes—they degrade the signal's credibility

## Real-World Example
**Education:** A degree from a demanding university signals intelligence and work ethic—not just knowledge. The years of effort and tuition are the costly signal. This is why prestige persists even when much of the content is forgettable.

**Charitable giving:** Public donations signal both altruism and wealth. The more you give away, the stronger the signal that you can afford to.

**Initiation rituals:** Military units, fraternities, and gangs demand painful or humiliating initiations. Only genuinely committed members would endure them—that's what makes them effective filters.

**Luxury goods:** A $50,000 watch is a costly signal of wealth. If counterfeits were perfect, the signal would collapse. The expensive authentication systems around luxury goods exist to preserve the signal's cost.

## Common Pitfalls
- Mistaking the costly signal for the underlying quality (the degree ≠ the skill)
- Arms races where everyone escalates signal costs without anyone gaining advantage
- Conflating cost with value—something can be costly to produce and worthless in itself
- Not recognizing that AI and automation are eroding the cost of many formerly credible signals
