## Definition
Actions or attributes that communicate information about someone's qualities, abilities, or intentions. Effective signals are hard to fake, making them credible indicators of underlying traits.

## Key Insight
Talk is cheap; actions reveal truth. When you can't directly verify someone's qualities (intelligence, commitment, wealth), you look for signals—costly actions that only someone with those qualities would take. Understanding signaling helps you read people better and signal your own value effectively.

## How to Apply
Reading signals:
- "What is this action signaling?"
- "How costly is this signal?" (More costly = more credible)
- "Could someone fake this signal?" (If yes, discount it)

Sending signals:
- Actions speak louder than words
- Choose signals that are hard for competitors to fake
- Signal what matters to your audience

## Real-World Example
**Education as signal**: A college degree signals intelligence, conscientiousness, and ability to complete difficult tasks—not just specific knowledge. Employers value degrees partly for the signal. Getting a degree from Harvard is an expensive, hard-to-fake signal that you're smart and hardworking.

**Luxury goods**: A $10,000 watch doesn't tell time better than a $100 watch. It signals wealth and [[Status Games|status]]. The high price makes it hard to fake, creating a credible signal. If Rolex watches were $50, they'd lose signaling value.

**Wedding rings**: Expensive engagement rings signal commitment. The cost (deliberately wasted money) shows you're serious about the relationship. Cheap rings would signal less commitment because they're easier to give.

**Working late at the office**: Staying late signals dedication—whether or not you're productive. Employees signal commitment through visible effort, even if working from home would be more efficient.

**Peacock's tail**: Classic biological example. Peacocks with elaborate tails signal "I'm so fit I can survive despite this handicap." The tail's cost (makes them vulnerable) is what makes it a credible signal of genetic fitness.

## Common Pitfalls
- Confusing signals with actual value (degrees don't guarantee skill)
- Missing that you're sending signals you don't intend
- Over-investing in signals at the expense of substance
- Not recognizing when others are signaling vs. when they're being genuine
