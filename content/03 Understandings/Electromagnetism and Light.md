Similar to gravity, Electromagnetic radiation (EM) is a fundamental property of nature. EM is not a single force, but a spectrum. Light is a small part of this spectrum that can be directly experienced by humans. Most of the EM spectrum is blocked by the Earth’s atmosphere. Since only light can penetrate down to the earth, humans and most animals have evolved to experience these directly.

### What causes Electromagnetism

To understand the origin/ cause of EM, we need to understand the structure of atoms. The typical atom consists of several types of particles, a number of which have not only mass but an additional property called electric charge. In the nucleus (central part) of every atom are protons, which are positively charged; outside the nucleus are electrons, which have a negative charge. In the vicinity of an electron charge, another charge feels a force of attraction or repulsion: opposite charges attract; like charges repel. When charges are not in motion, we observe only this electric attraction or repulsion. If charges are in motion, however (as they are inside every atom and in a wire carrying a current), then we measure another force called magnetism. 

While we've been familiar with Magnetism for long, its cause was not understood until the nineteenth century. 
1. Experiments with electric charges demonstrated that magnetism was the result of moving charged particles. Sometimes, the motion is clear, as in the coils of heavy wire that make an industrial electromagnet. Other times, it is more subtle, as in the kind of magnet you buy in a hardware store, in which many of the electrons inside the atoms are spinning in roughly the same direction; it is the alignment of their motion that causes the material to become magnetic.
2. Further experiments showed that changing magnetic fields could produce electric currents (and thus changing electric fields), and changing electric currents could in turn produce changing magnetic fields. So once begun, electric and magnetic field changes could continue to trigger each other.

Maxwell discovered that when electric charges change direction and/or speed, they produce a magnetic field. And a moving magnetic field produces electric field. In such a way, they continue propagating each other. He was able to calculate that the speed at which an electromagnetic disturbance moves through space is equal to the speed of light. On that basis, he speculated that light was one form of a family of possible electromagnetic disturbances called electromagnetic radiation. When light (reflected from the pages of an astronomy textbook, for example) enters a human eye, its changing electric and magnetic fields stimulate nerve endings, which then transmit the information contained in these changing fields to the brain.

### Electromagnetism as a Wave

Wave is a repeating phenomenon. Whether it is the up-and-down motion of a water wave or the changing electric and magnetic fields in a wave of light, the pattern of disturbance repeats in a cyclical way. Thus, any wave motion can be easily described by a series of crests and troughs. 

1. Wavelength: Moving from one crest through a trough to the next crest completes one cycle. The horizontal length covered by one cycle is called the wavelength. For example, in ocean waves, distance that separates successive wave crests is a wavelength.
2. Frequency: We can also characterise different waves by their frequency, the number of wave cycles that pass by per second. If you count 10 crests moving by each second, for example, then the frequency is 10 cycles per second (cps). In honour of Heinrich Hertz, the physicist who—inspired by Maxwell’s work—discovered radio waves, a cps is also called a hertz (Hz). 

>[!Info] Relationship between Wavelength and Frequency
Wavelength (λ) and frequency (f) are related because all electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed. Imagine a parade in which everyone is forced by prevailing traffic conditions to move at exactly the same speed. You stand on a corner and watch the waves of marchers come by. First you see row after row of miniature ponies. Because they are not very large and, therefore, have a shorter wavelength, a good number of the ponies can move past you each minute; we can say they have a high frequency. Next, however, come several rows of circus elephants. The elephants are large and marching at the same speed as the ponies, so far fewer of them can march past you per minute: Because they have a wider spacing (longer wavelength), they represent a lower frequency.

The formula for this relationship can be expressed as follows: for any wave motion, the speed at which a wave moves equals the frequency times the wavelength. Waves with longer wavelengths have lower frequencies. Mathematically, we can express this as

$$
\LARGE
c=λf
$$
where the Greek letter λ (lambda) is used to denote wavelength and c is the scientific symbol for the speed of light. Solving for the wavelength, this is expressed as:
$$
\LARGE
λ=cf
$$
EMs with the highest frequency are known as Gamma Rays, which are produced by astronomical events. Next comes X-rays and UV rays, followed by the visible spectrum. After visible spectrum comes IR rays, followed by microwaves. Then comes a large section of the spectrum, broadly called radio waves. These include AM, radar wave-lengths, etc. 

Each section of the spectrum have their own unique qualities. For e.g., Gamma Rays have such high frequency that they can be harmful for human flesh and bone. X-Rays can penetrate human flesh, but not bone. Microwaves are absorbed by water vapour, but nothing much else. AM waves are reflected back by the Ionosphere surrounding earth, making them ideal for transmission. 

For visible light, our eyes perceive different wavelengths as different colors: red, for example, is the longest visible wavelength, and violet is the shortest. Other invisible forms of electromagnetic radiation have different wavelengths, as we will see in the next section.
 
### Electromagnetism as Particle

Niels Bohr also figured out that EM waves can be explained through particles. Thus, EM has what is called a wave-particle duality.

Every element is made up of atoms. Each atom has a certain number of electrons revolving around a nucleus that contains positively-charged protons and neutral neutrons. As per Bohr’s theory, the electrons are only allowed to revolve around the nucleus in certain fixed orbits (or energy states). Moving from one energy state to another is possible, but only if the electron releases (as photon) or captures the required energy for it. And for atoms of every different element, this ‘required energy’ is different. So, when white light passes through a gas, and some of it is absorbed, inspecting the resultant spectrum (known as absorption spectrum) and figuring out what wavelengths are missing, let’s us identify what the intermediary gas is made of. 

Hence, each photon that gets released or is absorbed, carries a certain amount of energy that is proportional to the frequency (f) of the wave it represents. The value of its energy (E) is given by the formula:
$$
\LARGE
E = hf
$$
*where, h is the constant of prioritionality, called Planck's contant; it has a value of 6.626 x 10<sup>-34</sup> joule-seconds (J-s)*

These properties of EM spectra lend itself to various uses in Astronomy:

### Redshift:

When the source of EM moves away or towards you, the spectrum shifts. For example, when the source moves away from you, the waves become spread out (i.e. their wave length seemingly becomes higher) and shifts towards the red side of the spectrum. When the source comes towards you, the waves become more condensed, and hence said to be blue-shifted (or violet-shifted). 

![[Redshift.png]]

Using this information, we can guess that all distant astronomical galaxies, who’s spectrums seem red-shifted, are moving away from us. This is assumed to be proof that the universe is expanding.

### Inverse-Square Law

The intensity of EM radiation falls off with distance, similar to gravity. As light travels away from a source, it has to cover an ever-widening space; yet the total amount of light available can't change since the lift has already left the source. The increase in the area that the light must cover is proportional to the square of the distance that the light has travelled. If we stand twice as far from the source, our eyes will intercept two-squared (2 x 2), or four times less light. If we stand 10-times farther from the source, we get 10-squared, or 100 times less light. This is known as the inverse square law and is applicable for gravity as well.

### Radiation Laws - Relationship with Temperature:

![[Temp. vs. Emission.png]]

#### First Law (or, Planck's Law): 
Planck's Law defines how the intensity of radiation is distributed across wavelengths for a given temperature. 

According to this law:

- Hotter objects emit more radiation at all wavelengths.  This is because it invariably has molecules and atoms that move about slower or faster than the average speed. Hence, it emits radiation (photons) at all wavelengths (color). 
- As the temperature increases, the peak of the radiation shifts to shorter wavelengths (higher energy).
#### Second Law (or, Stefan-Boltzman's Law)
**Stefan-Boltzmann's Law** tells us the total energy emitted by a black body across all wavelengths, but it doesn't specify how this energy is distributed over different wavelengths. At higher temperatures, any object will emit more radiation. This is because at higher temperatures, atoms will be more agitated and hence will collide more.

This is encapsulated in Stefan-Boltzman’s law

$$
\LARGE
F = \sigma * T^4
$$

Here, 
*σ = 5.67 x 10<sup>-8</sup>, and 
F = Energy flux, or measure of power per square meter (To figure out total power emitted by a star, multiply it by its surface area, i.e. 4πR^2)* 

#### Third Law (or, Wien's Law)
Wein's Law, also known as **Wien's Displacement Law**, describes the relationship between the temperature of a blackbody and the wavelength at which it emits the most radiation. Essentially, as the temperature of an object increases, the peak wavelength of its emitted radiation shifts to shorter wavelengths.  ^3fb19d

The law is mathematically expressed as:

$$
\LARGE
\lambda (max) = \frac{3 * 10^6}{T}
$$
Where:
- $\lambda$ is the peak wavelength (in meters),
- T is the absolute temperature of the blackbody (in kelvins),

Key Takeaways:
1. **Hotter objects emit shorter wavelengths**: As an object gets hotter, it shifts from emitting infrared radiation to visible light, and eventually ultraviolet as the temperature increases further.
2. **Cooler objects emit longer wavelengths**: Cooler objects emit radiation at longer wavelengths, such as in the infrared or radio range.

For example, the Sun, with a surface temperature of about 5,778 K, emits most of its radiation in the visible spectrum, whereas a cooler object, like a human body (~310 K), emits primarily in the infrared. 

Wein's Law helps astronomers determine the temperature of stars and other celestial bodies by analyzing the light they emit. For e.g., for our Sun, the wavelength at which the maximum energy is emitted is 520 nm. Using this, we can calculate the temperature of the sun to be 5769 K.
