## Definition
Considering not just the immediate consequences of a decision, but also the consequences of those consequences.

## Key Insight
First-order thinking asks "What happens next?" Second-order thinking asks "And then what?" Most people stop at first-order effects, which creates opportunities for those who think deeper.

## How to Apply
For any decision, ask yourself:
- What happens immediately?
- Then what happens?
- And after that?
- Who else is affected and how will they respond?

## Real-World Example
A city has too many deer eating residents' gardens. First-order thinking: "Let's introduce wolves to control the deer population." Second-order thinking reveals: Wolves will reduce deer, but they'll also threaten pets, livestock, and potentially children. This might cause people to move away, reducing tax revenue. People might hunt the wolves, creating conflict. The ecosystem might shift in unexpected ways.

## Common Pitfalls
- Analysis paralysis from overthinking simple decisions
- Assuming you can predict all consequences (you can't)
- Ignoring first-order effects in pursuit of deeper thinking
