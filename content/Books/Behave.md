# The Physiology of the Neuron and the Biological Foundations of Behavior

## Introduction

The neuron is the fundamental physiological unit of the brain. Its structure, electrochemical properties, and capacity for plastic change constitute the immediate substrate from which behavior emerges. Any serious account of human action must therefore begin at the level of membrane potentials, ion gradients, synaptic transmission, and network integration.

Your source material outlines the essential structural and signaling features of neurons. What follows is an expanded physiological treatment, integrated with Sapolsky’s multi-layered analysis of behavior in _Behave_, which situates neuronal events within broader temporal and biological contexts.

# I. Structural Organization of the Neuron

Neurons are structurally polarized cells designed for directional information flow .

## 1. Gross Morphology

### Dendrites

- Receive synaptic input.
    
- Contain ligand-gated ion channels.
    
- Often bear dendritic spines, which are dynamic structures involved in learning.
    

### Soma (Cell Body)

- Houses nucleus and ribosomes.
    
- Site of protein synthesis.
    
- Integrates graded potentials arriving from dendrites.

- Decision zone.

### Axon

- Conducts action potentials.
    
- May be myelinated or unmyelinated.
    

### Axon Terminals

- Contain synaptic vesicles.
    
- Release neurotransmitters.
    

Sapolsky emphasizes that structure constrains computation. Dendritic complexity affects integration. Axonal length and myelination affect behavioral timing. Morphology is computational architecture.

# II. Basic Neurophysiology: Membrane Potential

At rest, neurons maintain a voltage difference across their membrane, typically about –70 mV .

## 1. Ion Gradients

Key ions:

- Sodium (Na⁺) high outside.
    
- Potassium (K⁺) high inside.
    
- Chloride (Cl⁻) variable.
    
- Calcium (Ca²⁺) very low inside.
    

Maintained by:

- Sodium–potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase).
    
    - Pumps 3 Na⁺ out.
        
    - Pumps 2 K⁺ in.
        
    - Consumes ATP.
        

This creates electrochemical gradients that allow rapid electrical signaling.

---

## 2. Resting Potential

The membrane is selectively permeable, especially to K⁺ through leak channels. Potassium efflux leaves behind negatively charged proteins, generating a negative resting potential.

```
Outside:  + + + + + (Na+ rich)
---------------------------------
Membrane
---------------------------------
Inside:   - - - - - (K+ rich)
```

The resting potential represents stored potential energy.

# III. The Action Potential

When depolarization reaches threshold (~ –55 mV), voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open.

## Sequence

```
1. Resting:        -70 mV
2. Threshold:      -55 mV
3. Depolarization: Na+ influx
4. Peak:           +40 mV
5. Repolarization: K+ efflux
6. Hyperpolarization
7. Return to rest
```

### Key Properties

- All-or-nothing .
    
- Self-propagating.
    
- Unidirectional (refractory period prevents backward firing).
    

Information is encoded in:

- Firing frequency.
    
- Timing.
    
- Pattern synchrony.
    

Sapolsky situates this millisecond event within broader influences:

- Stress hormones alter membrane excitability.
    
- Dopamine alters threshold likelihood.
    
- Development affects channel distribution.
    

The spike is immediate; its causes are layered.


# The Neuron as the Biological Substrate of Behavior

### From Cellular Architecture to Moral Action

## Introduction

Any serious account of behavior must begin at the level of the neuron. The neuron is not merely a signaling cell but the fundamental computational unit of the nervous system. Its architecture, electrochemical dynamics, and plastic properties form the physical substrate from which perception, memory, emotion, and moral decision-making emerge. The structural and functional features described in your source material provide the essential biological groundwork. However, as Sapolsky argues in _Behave_, neuronal activity cannot be understood in isolation from temporal layering, hormonal modulation, developmental shaping, and evolutionary history. To understand behavior is to situate the neuron within nested scales of causation.

This essay proceeds from structure to signaling, from synapse to system, and from microsecond firing events to the deep temporal scaffolding that shapes action.

## I. Neuronal Architecture and Functional Asymmetry

Neurons differ radically from most somatic cells in both morphology and purpose. While typical cells are roughly spherical and locally self-contained, neurons are elongated and polarized . This asymmetry is not incidental. It encodes directionality.

The dendrites constitute the receptive domain of the neuron. Their branching arborization maximizes surface area, permitting the reception of thousands of synaptic inputs. The soma integrates these signals, and the axon transmits the resultant output, sometimes across remarkable distances. In this geometry, form mirrors function: convergence at dendrites, integration at the soma, divergence at axon terminals.

Sapolsky emphasizes that this structural polarity is the first layer of constraint on behavior. The architecture of a neuron limits what computations are possible. Dendritic complexity influences input integration. Axonal myelination determines conduction speed. Thus, even before neurotransmitters are considered, physical morphology shapes behavioral latency and processing capacity.

Neural structure is therefore both anatomical and computational.

## II. Electrical Excitability and the Logic of the Action Potential

At rest, the neuronal membrane maintains a voltage differential of approximately –70 mV . This resting potential is not passive but metabolically maintained through ion gradients. When excitatory inputs sufficiently depolarize the membrane to threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open, initiating an action potential.

The action potential is all-or-nothing. Its amplitude does not scale with stimulus intensity. Information is encoded not in magnitude but in frequency and pattern.

This binary firing property introduces a critical principle: individual neurons operate digitally, yet networks behave analogically. A neuron either fires or does not. However, the probability of firing across populations yields graded responses.

Sapolsky situates this millisecond event within a broader causal chain. One second before a behavioral act, certain neurons fire. But seconds before that firing, neurotransmitter concentrations modulate membrane excitability. Hours before that, hormonal states alter receptor density. Years before, developmental processes determined synaptic density. The action potential is therefore the final execution step in a temporally stratified cascade.

To isolate the spike is to ignore the history embedded within it.

## III. The Synapse and Chemical Modulation

Neurons do not directly touch; they communicate across synaptic clefts . When an action potential reaches the axon terminal, calcium influx triggers vesicular release of neurotransmitters. These molecules bind to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, generating excitatory or inhibitory potentials.

Chemical transmission introduces variability. Unlike the fixed amplitude of the action potential, synaptic signaling can be modulated in strength, duration, and probability. This permits plasticity and contextual responsiveness.

Sapolsky resists simplistic transmitter-behavior correlations. Dopamine is not “pleasure,” but rather encodes prediction error and motivational salience. Serotonin does not simply regulate mood but influences impulse control and social hierarchy processing. Glutamate drives excitation but can also induce excitotoxic damage under pathological conditions. Context, receptor subtype, and circuit location determine effect.

Thus, neurotransmitters do not cause behaviors; they bias networks toward certain computational states.

## IV. Conduction Velocity and Functional Differentiation

Axonal diameter and myelination determine signal velocity . Myelin permits saltatory conduction, dramatically increasing transmission speed. Fast-conducting neurons subserve reflexes and survival circuits. Slower, unmyelinated pathways often participate in modulatory or diffuse signaling.

Sapolsky underscores that behavioral systems are hierarchically layered partly through conduction differences. Rapid subcortical circuits generate immediate responses. Slower cortical circuits allow regulation and reinterpretation. This temporal disparity is central to understanding impulse and restraint.

Speed is not merely mechanical; it is behavioral architecture.

## V. Plasticity and the Encoding of Experience

The principle that “neurons that fire together wire together” captures the logic of synaptic strengthening . Long-term potentiation involves increased receptor insertion, structural spine growth, and altered gene expression. Conversely, synaptic pruning eliminates underused connections.

Sapolsky integrates plasticity with lived experience. Chronic stress shrinks dendritic arbors in the hippocampus while expanding them in the amygdala. Enriched environments increase synaptic density. Early trauma alters receptor distributions.

Plasticity ensures that neurons are not static hardware but history-sensitive processors. Each synapse reflects prior co-activation patterns. The adult brain is therefore an archive of repeated experience encoded in microstructure.

Learning is structural revision.

## VI. Network Integration and Emergent Cognition

Individual neurons perform limited computations. The complexity of cognition arises from networks.

Each neuron integrates thousands of excitatory and inhibitory inputs . Spatial and temporal summation determine firing probability. Across populations, distributed representations encode memories and concepts.

Associative thinking arises because overlapping neural populations participate in multiple representations. The neural circuitry involved in grasping a physical object overlaps partially with that engaged when grasping an abstract concept. Metaphor is thus not purely linguistic but neurobiological.

Sapolsky frames this in systemic terms: the amygdala processes threat, the hippocampus encodes context, the prefrontal cortex mediates regulation. Behavior emerges from their interaction. No region or neuron is sovereign.

The neuron is necessary, but networks are explanatory.

## VII. Temporal Stratification and the Biology of Action

The distinctive contribution of _Behave_ is its insistence on temporal layering.

One second before action: neuronal firing patterns.  
Minutes before: neuromodulator tone.  
Hours before: hormonal states such as cortisol or testosterone.  
Months to years before: synaptic remodeling.  
Early life: developmental wiring.  
Evolutionary time: species-specific architecture.

Neurons operate within these layers simultaneously. The spike reflects ion channel dynamics; those channels reflect protein expression; that expression reflects genetic regulation shaped by experience; that experience unfolds within cultural and evolutionary constraints.

Behavior is thus not reducible to neurons, but neither can it bypass them.

## Conclusion

The neuron is an asymmetric, electrically excitable, chemically modulated, plastic computational unit . Its dendrites receive, its soma integrates, its axon transmits. Action potentials propagate in binary fashion, while synapses permit graded modulation. Plasticity encodes experience, and networks generate emergent cognition.

Sapolsky’s broader framework compels us to view these processes within layered causation. Neurons do not simply fire; they fire within endocrine states, developmental histories, and evolutionary architectures. The apparent immediacy of behavior conceals deep temporal roots.

The neuron, therefore, is neither a mere switch nor a metaphysical agent. It is a dynamic, context-sensitive biological processor whose activity, embedded within networks and time, produces the phenomena we label thought, emotion, and moral action.

Behavior is the final expression of neuronal events structured by biology across scales.
