# Interoception: Notes from "How Emotions are Made"

## Core Concept

According to Lisa Feldman Barrett in _How Emotions are Made_, interoception is the brain's perception of sensations from within the body—signals like a racing heart or stomach ache that the brain must make sense of through prediction, before they are interpreted as specific emotions.The brain is locked inside the skull, making the body just another external stimulus source. The brain must make sense of interoceptive cues (like a dull ache in the stomach) through **prediction**, as any single cue could have multiple possible causes.

## How We Experience Interoception

- **Generally**: Broad feelings of pleasure, displeasure, arousal, or calmness
- **Intensely**: Specific moments experienced as emotions

**Key distinction**: Interoception is the stimulus the brain perceives from the inner body, while the interoceptive network is the organ that enables this perception.

## The Interoceptive Network: Two Distinct Roles

### 1. Body Budgeting (BB)

Sends predictions to the body to control the internal environment

> Examples: speed up heart rate, slow down breathing, release hormones

### 2. Primary Interoceptive Cortex (PIC)

Represents sensations inside your body

## How They Work Together

Every time the BB region predicts a change, it also predicts the **sensory consequences** of that change:

**Example**:

- Prediction: increase heart rate
- Predicted sensation: pounding feeling

These are called **interoceptive predictions**, which flow to the PIC where they are simulated. The PIC also receives actual signals from the body and matches them against the simulations from the BB region.

## Concrete Example: Seeing Your Boss

1. **You see your boss** (visual stimulus)
2. **BB region makes a prediction** based on your belief that she judges you: "I will need energy for this situation"
3. **BB sends commands to the body**: releases cortisol, pumps glucose
4. **Physical changes occur in the body**: increased heart rate, glucose in bloodstream, etc.
5. **Two things happen simultaneously**:
    - BB sends **interoceptive predictions** to PIC ("you should feel a pounding sensation, energy surge")
    - The **actual body** sends **real sensory signals** to PIC ("here's what's actually happening")
6. **PIC compares** the prediction vs. reality

So the BB is like a manager that both:
- **Does the work** (controls the body)
- **Predicts the outcome** (tells PIC what to expect)

Then PIC checks if the prediction matched reality.

## The Processing Chain

```
Interoception (perceiving the stimulus)
    ↓
Affect (how it feels)
    ↓
Concepts (how it's interpreted)
    ↓
Emotion (how it's experienced)
```

**Critical point**: Interoception is purely about perceiving the stimulus, NOT about how it's interpreted. The interpretation happens through conceptual processing, which transforms affect into experienced emotion.

When there's a mismatch between:

- What BB predicted (the simulation sent to PIC)
- What actually happened (the real signals from the body)

This creates what's called a **prediction error**.

## What Happens Next

The brain has to resolve this mismatch. Based on the predictive processing framework that this book uses, there are generally two possibilities:

### 1. **Update the prediction** (Learning)

The brain says "my prediction was wrong" and adjusts its model for next time. This is how we learn and refine our understanding of what different body states mean.

### 2. **Adjust the body to match the prediction** (Active inference)

The brain doubles down on its prediction and sends stronger signals to make the body conform to what it expected. Essentially: "I predicted this sensation, so let's make it happen."

### 3. **Reinterpret the signals** (Re-conceptualization)

The brain might use different concepts to make sense of the same interoceptive sensations. For example, the same physical arousal could be interpreted as "anxiety" in one context or "excitement" in another.

## The Key Insight

The brain is constantly trying to minimize prediction error. It's always working to make predictions match reality - either by updating its predictions or by changing the body to fit the predictions.

This is why context matters so much for emotions - the same physical sensations can be experienced completely differently depending on what the brain predicts should be happening.

## Scenario: Heart beating fast while watching TV

### What happens:

1. **Your heart is beating fast** (actual physical state)
2. **Body sends signals to PIC**: "Heart is pounding"
3. **BB also sends predictions to PIC**: But here's the key question - **what does BB predict?**

### BB's Prediction Depends on Context:

**BB is constantly making predictions based on:**

- What you're doing (watching TV)
- What's on screen (action scene? romantic scene? news?)
- Your past experiences
- Your current body budget state

### Two Different Scenarios:

**Scenario A: You're watching a thriller**

- **BB predicts**: "We need energy for this tense situation" → already predicted the fast heartbeat
- **BB sends to PIC**: "Expect pounding heart, tension"
- **PIC receives from body**: "Heart pounding"
- **Match!** → PIC experiences this as affect (unpleasant + high arousal)
- **Concept applied**: Given context (thriller), brain categorizes as "suspense" or "anxiety"
- **Emotion experienced**: Fear/tension

**Scenario B: You're watching a boring show**

- **BB didn't predict**: No reason for fast heartbeat (maybe you drank too much coffee?)
- **BB sends to PIC**: "Expect calm, low arousal"
- **PIC receives from body**: "Heart pounding"
- **Mismatch!** → Prediction error
- **Brain scrambles to explain**: "Why is my heart racing?"
- **Concept applied**: Maybe "I'm anxious about something" or "I feel weird" or even "I'm excited about something I forgot"
- **Emotion experienced**: Free-floating anxiety, confusion, or the brain might even make you think of something to worry about to explain the sensation

### The Key Point:

**BB doesn't just react to the body - it's constantly predicting what SHOULD be happening based on context.** The emotion you experience depends on:

1. What sensations occur (interoception)
2. What BB predicted should occur
3. The concepts your brain uses to make sense of the match or mismatch

## Your Scenario:

- **BB predicts threat** (based on past associations with deserted paths)
- **BB sends to PIC**: "Expect racing heart, tension, adrenaline"
- **PIC receives from body**: "Everything is calm, no arousal"
- **Result**: Prediction error (mismatch)

## What Likely Happens:

Based on the predictive processing framework, the brain would probably:

### Option 1: **Active Inference - Make the body match the prediction**

- BB says "I predicted danger, so there MUST be danger"
- **BB actually triggers the physical changes**: releases cortisol, speeds up heart, etc.
- Now the body DOES send alarming signals to PIC
- Prediction becomes self-fulfilling

This is likely what happens in your example! The BB doesn't just passively predict - **it actively controls the body**. So if BB predicts threat, it will likely **create** the bodily response.
