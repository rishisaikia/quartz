## Definition
The design of environments in which people make choices. How options are presented, ordered, framed, or structured has a significant and predictable influence on the choices people make—independent of the options themselves.

## Key Insight
There is no neutral choice environment. Every system that presents options has an architecture, whether deliberately designed or not. The question is never "should we influence choices?" but "how are we influencing them, and toward what end?" Designers of choice environments are always, unavoidably, nudging.

## How to Apply
When designing systems:
- Identify what the most common or desirable choice is, then make it the default
- Place high-value options where attention naturally falls
- Reduce friction for beneficial choices; increase it for harmful ones
- Frame options in terms of what is gained, not just what is chosen

When making decisions:
- Ask: "How is this choice being presented to me, and by whom?"
- Look for defaults, ordering effects, and framing that may be nudging you
- Reconstruct the choice from scratch to counter architectural bias

## Real-World Example
**Organ donation:** Countries with opt-out systems (you're a donor unless you choose otherwise) have dramatically higher donation rates than opt-in systems. Same people, same underlying preferences—different architecture, radically different outcomes.

**Cafeteria design:** Placing healthy foods at eye level and at the front of the line dramatically increases their selection, without removing any options. Nudging without banning.

**Retirement savings:** Auto-enrollment in 401(k) plans with default contribution rates sharply increases retirement savings participation. Inertia works for you when the default is well-designed.

**Website checkout:** Long, multi-step checkouts reduce conversion. One-click purchasing increases it. Amazon's patent on one-click buying was, essentially, a patent on a choice architecture.

## Common Pitfalls
- Believing you can design a "neutral" choice environment—you can't
- Using choice architecture to exploit rather than help (dark patterns in UX)
- Ignoring how your choice environments are influencing people already
- Over-nudging to the point of removing meaningful autonomy
