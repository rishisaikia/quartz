## Definition
When choosing between competing explanations, the simplest one—requiring the fewest assumptions—is most likely to be correct.

## Key Insight
Complexity should not be added without necessity. Each additional assumption is a point of failure, a place where you could be wrong. Simpler explanations are more testable, more robust, and more often correct.

## How to Apply
When evaluating explanations or solutions:
- List the assumptions each requires
- Choose the one with fewest necessary assumptions
- Add complexity only when evidence demands it
- Ask: "What's the simplest explanation that fits the facts?"

## Real-World Example
Your car won't start. Possible explanations:
1. Dead battery (simple: one assumption)
2. Government surveillance device draining power through electromagnetic interference while aliens simultaneously disrupt electrical systems (complex: multiple wild assumptions)

The battery is likely dead. Occam's Razor doesn't guarantee this is correct, but it's the best place to start.

In medicine: A patient has a headache, fatigue, and muscle aches. One disease causing all three symptoms (like the flu) is more likely than three separate rare conditions coincidentally occurring together.

In business: Sales dropped 20% last month. Simple explanation: The website was down for 2 days. Complex explanation: Market shifts, competitor actions, seasonal variations, and cosmic rays all aligned. Start with the website.

## Common Pitfalls
- Using this to dismiss genuinely complex truths
- Confusing "simple explanation" with "explanation I prefer"
- Ignoring evidence because it makes things more complex
