## Definition
"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Once you optimize for a specific metric, people game the metric rather than improving the underlying thing it's supposed to measure.

## Key Insight
Metrics are proxies for what we actually care about. When we fixate on the metric and reward it, people optimize for the metric at the expense of the real goal. You get what you measure, but often not what you want.

## How to Apply
When setting metrics or targets:
- Ask: "Can this be gamed?"
- Ask: "What behaviors will this incentivize?"
- Use multiple metrics that are harder to game simultaneously
- Remember the metric is a proxy, not the goal
- Monitor for gaming and adjust

## Real-World Example
Soviet factories were measured on weight of nails produced. They made enormous, useless nails because that maximized weight. When the measure changed to number of nails, they made tiny, useless nails to maximize count. The metric was gamed; the real goal (useful nails) was ignored.

Schools measured by test scores teach to the test, narrowing education to test-taking skills rather than actual learning. The metric (test scores) replaced the goal (education).

Call centers measured by calls answered per hour incentivize hanging up quickly, not solving problems. Agents optimize the metric by rushing customers off the phone, degrading actual service quality.

Social media companies optimized for "engagement" (time on site, clicks, shares). This metric incentivized outrage, misinformation, and addiction because those drive engagement—even though they harm users and society.

## Common Pitfalls
- Abandoning all metrics because they can be gamed (some are still useful)
- Not updating metrics when gaming becomes apparent
- Punishing people for gaming metrics you poorly designed
- Confusing metric improvement with actual improvement
