## Definition
The tendency to see past events as having been more predictable than they actually were. "I knew it all along" bias.

## Key Insight
After something happens, our brains rewrite the past to make it seem obvious and inevitable. This makes us overconfident in our ability to predict the future and prevents us from learning from mistakes (because we think we "knew" what would happen anyway).

## How to Apply
Combat hindsight bias:
- Write down predictions before events occur
- Document your reasoning at the time of decisions
- After outcomes, ask "What did I actually expect beforehand?"
- Separate the quality of a decision from the quality of the outcome

## Real-World Example
Before the 2008 financial crisis, almost nobody predicted it. After the crash, countless people claimed "I knew the housing market was a bubble." They didn't—they're experiencing hindsight bias, rewriting their memory to seem prescient.

You watch a mystery movie. After the reveal, you think "Of course! All the clues were there!" You rewatch it and realize the clues were ambiguous—you couldn't have figured it out, you just feel like you could have because you now know the answer.

A startup fails. Everyone says "It was obvious they would fail—bad idea, wrong market, poor execution." But these same people didn't predict failure before (some even invested). Hindsight bias makes the outcome seem predictable.

In medicine: A patient has unusual symptoms. After diagnosis, doctors say "Classic presentation of X disease." But before the diagnosis, it was ambiguous and could have been many conditions.

## Common Pitfalls
- Judging past decisions by their outcomes rather than by what was known at the time
- Becoming overconfident in predictions because past events seem obvious
- Not learning from mistakes because you think you "knew better"
- Being too harsh on others for not predicting things that seem obvious in hindsight
