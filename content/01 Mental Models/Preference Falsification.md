## Definition
The act of misrepresenting one's genuine preferences to conform to perceived social norms or avoid social consequences. People publicly express views they don't privately hold.

## Key Insight
When the cost of expressing your true preference is high, people lie—to others and eventually to themselves. This creates a gap between private and public opinion that can persist for decades. Then suddenly, when conditions change, the dam breaks and "unexpected" revolutions happen.

## How to Apply
Recognize preference falsification:
- Look for gaps between private conversations and public statements
- Notice topics where people change their tune depending on who's listening
- Ask: "What would people say if there were no social consequences?"
- Be skeptical of unanimous public support (it might be falsified)

When you're falsifying: Recognize the cost to your authenticity and society.

## Real-World Example
Soviet Union collapse: For decades, citizens publicly supported communism while privately opposing it. Western observers saw public displays of support and thought the system was stable. In reality, almost everyone was falsifying their preferences. When Gorbachev's reforms made dissent safer, the facade collapsed almost overnight—the sudden "shift" in public opinion was actually just the end of mass preference falsification.

Workplace dynamics: Employees publicly praise a terrible new policy in meetings, then privately complain about it. Management thinks everyone loves it and doubles down. No one wants to be the first to object publicly.

Social media: People post opinions they think will get approval rather than what they actually believe. This creates false consensus and amplifies certain views while suppressing others.

Historical example: Many Germans privately opposed Nazi policies but publicly supported them out of fear. This preference falsification made the regime seem more popular than it was, which in turn intimidated more people into falsifying their preferences—a reinforcing cycle.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming public consensus reflects private beliefs
- Underestimating how many people are falsifying preferences
- Falsifying your own preferences so long you lose touch with what you truly believe
- Not recognizing when a sudden "shift" in opinion is actually just the end of falsification
