## Definition
The neurological process by which behaviors become automatic through repetition, reducing the mental effort required to perform them.

## Key Insight
Your brain loves efficiency. Repeated behaviors get encoded into automatic routines, freeing up mental energy. This is powerful for good habits (exercise, reading) and dangerous for bad ones (scrolling, snacking). The key is understanding the cue-routine-reward loop.

## How to Apply
Build good habits by:
- Making the cue obvious (lay out gym clothes)
- Making the routine easy (start with 5 minutes)
- Making the reward immediate (track it, celebrate small wins)
- Repeat consistently until it feels automatic (21-66 days typically)

Break bad habits by disrupting the cue or changing the routine.

## Real-World Example
You want to exercise daily. Don't rely on willpower. Instead: Put your gym shoes by the bed (cue). When you wake up, put them on immediately (routine). Track your workout and feel accomplished (reward). After 30 days, putting on gym shoes when you wake up becomes automatic—you feel weird NOT doing it.

Compare this to "I'll exercise when I feel motivated." Motivation is unreliable. Habits don't require motivation once established.

Breaking a bad habit: You habitually check social media when bored. The cue is "boredom," the routine is "open phone," the reward is "stimulation." To break it: When bored (cue), go for a short walk instead (new routine), get the stimulation from movement (reward). Eventually, boredom triggers walking, not scrolling.

## Common Pitfalls
- Trying to change too many habits at once
- Not making the habit easy enough to stick during hard days
- Expecting motivation to do the work that routine should do
- Giving up after one missed day instead of just continuing
