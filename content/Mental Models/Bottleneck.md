## Definition
The single point in a process that limits the overall throughput or performance of the entire system. The weakest link that constrains everything else.

## Key Insight
A chain is only as strong as its weakest link. Improving non-bottleneck parts doesn't improve the system—only improving the bottleneck matters. Identifying and addressing the bottleneck is usually the highest-leverage improvement you can make.

## How to Apply
Find the bottleneck:
- "What's the slowest step in this process?"
- "What determines maximum throughput?"
- "Where does work pile up waiting?"
- "If I could improve only one thing, what would have the biggest impact?"

Address it:
- Increase bottleneck capacity
- Remove bottleneck entirely
- Work around it
- Only then optimize other parts

## Real-World Example
**Manufacturing**: A factory can produce 100 units/hour in every step except Quality Control, which can only handle 50 units/hour. The factory's max output is 50 units/hour. Making production faster won't help—QC is the bottleneck. Only speeding up QC increases total output.

**Restaurants**: The kitchen can make 100 meals/hour but only has 20 tables. Tables are the bottleneck—faster cooking won't serve more customers. Adding tables (or increasing table turnover) is the only way to grow.

**Personal productivity**: You can respond to emails super fast, but if writing is your bottleneck (slow writing limits output), faster email won't help. You must improve writing speed or output stays constrained.

**Highway traffic**: One narrow section of highway limits traffic flow for the entire route. Making other parts wider doesn't help. The bottleneck section determines maximum throughput.

**Sales pipeline**: Marketing generates 1000 leads/month, sales can contact 500/month, but customer success can only onboard 50 new clients/month. Onboarding is the bottleneck. Getting more leads or sales capacity won't grow revenue—only expanding onboarding will.

## Common Pitfalls
- Optimizing non-bottleneck steps (wasted effort)
- Not identifying the true bottleneck (optimizing the wrong thing)
- Fixing one bottleneck only to create a new one elsewhere
- Assuming the bottleneck is permanent (it can shift as you grow)
- Overinvesting in bottleneck when simpler workarounds exist
