## Definition
We think differently about distant/abstract things (far mode: idealistic, principled, socially conscious) versus immediate/concrete things (near mode: practical, selfish, detail-oriented). Both modes are genuine, which is why we're not simply lying when our actions contradict our stated values.

## Key Insight
The gap between what we say and what we do isn't hypocrisy—it's mode switching. In far mode (thinking about distant future, abstract principles, hypotheticals), we sincerely endorse noble values. In near mode (facing concrete decisions, immediate choices), we act on practical, self-interested motives. Both are real, neither is fake.

## How to Apply
Recognize mode switching:
- "Am I thinking abstractly (far) or concretely (near)?"
- "Do my far-mode values match near-mode actions?"
- "What changes when I shift from abstract to concrete?"

Bridge the gap:
- Make far-mode commitments concrete and immediate
- Recognize that others' far-mode statements might not predict near-mode behavior
- Don't judge people solely on their abstract principles or concrete actions—both matter

## Real-World Example
**Diet and exercise:**
- Far mode: "I value health and will exercise regularly"
- Near mode: "I'm tired today, I'll skip the gym"
You sincerely value health (far mode), but when facing the concrete choice (near mode), comfort wins. Neither is fake—you really do value both health and comfort, just weighted differently in different modes.

**Charity and altruism:**
- Far mode: "I care deeply about poverty and will donate generously"
- Near mode: "That's expensive, maybe later"
You genuinely care about poverty (far mode), but when facing the concrete cost (near mode), self-interest dominates. Both sentiments are real.

**Environmental values:**
- Far mode: "Climate change is critical, we must act"
- Near mode: "Flying to this vacation is worth it"
You sincerely believe climate matters (far mode), but in concrete choice (near mode), personal enjoyment wins. You're not lying in either mode.

**Political principles:**
- Far mode: "I believe in free speech, even for views I hate"
- Near mode: "Actually, this specific speech should be banned"
You genuinely endorse free speech (far mode), but when confronting specific offensive speech (near mode), you want it stopped. Both reactions are real.

**Hiring and meritocracy:**
- Far mode: "We should hire the most qualified candidate"
- Near mode: "But this candidate is my friend's kid"
You sincerely believe in meritocracy (far mode), but when making concrete hiring decision (near mode), personal relationships matter.

## Common Pitfalls
- Judging yourself by far-mode intentions while judging others by near-mode actions
- Assuming stated values (far mode) predict behavior (near mode)
- Calling people hypocrites for mode-switching (it's universal, not moral failing)
- Not recognizing when you're in far vs near mode
- Making commitments in far mode that you can't keep in near mode

## Notes
In [[The Elephant in the Brain]], Robin Hanson and Kevin Simler use near/far thinking (Construal Level Theory) to explain the gap between our stated values and actual behavior. This is one of their key frameworks for understanding how we can be simultaneously sincere and self-deceived.

**The Psychological Distance Dimension:**
Far mode activates when thinking about:
- Distant future
- Distant places
- Other people
- Hypothetical scenarios
- Abstract concepts
- Why questions

Near mode activates when thinking about:
- Immediate present
- Here and now
- Yourself
- Concrete situations
- Specific details
- How questions

**Why We're Not Lying:**
The book emphasizes that both modes reflect genuine attitudes. When you say "I care about the poor" (far mode), you're not lying. When you walk past a homeless person (near mode), you're not betraying that value. You're operating in different mental modes that weight factors differently.

This resolves the puzzle of how we can simultaneously hold noble values and act selfishly. The values are sincere (far mode) and the selfishness is rational (near mode). The [[Press Secretary Brain]] reports our far-mode values as our "real" motives because they're more socially acceptable.

**Political Beliefs:**
Hanson and Simler apply this extensively to politics. In far mode, we endorse abstract principles: equality, freedom, justice, fairness. These are genuine beliefs. But in near mode, facing concrete policy questions, we respond tribally, selfishly, and practically.

This explains why people maintain strong political opinions on issues they know nothing about. Far mode doesn't require detailed knowledge—it operates on abstract principles and tribal affiliation. Near mode requires specifics, but we rarely face concrete political decisions personally.

**Social Signaling in Far Mode:**
The book argues that far-mode thinking is particularly important for social signaling. When you express idealistic principles, you're signaling your values to others. You sincerely believe these principles (they're not fake), but they exist primarily in far mode where they don't conflict with near-mode practical constraints.

Example: You sincerely believe "everyone deserves healthcare." This far-mode value signals compassion. But when facing the near-mode question "should my taxes increase by $5,000 for universal healthcare?" you might balk. Both reactions are genuine.

**Construal Level Theory Application:**
Though the near/far framework comes from Robin Hanson's earlier work more than the book itself, it underlies the entire analysis. The authors explain institutions like education, medicine, and politics by showing how:
- Far-mode: We endorse noble purposes (learning, healing, good governance)
- Near-mode: We pursue practical purposes (credentials, care signaling, tribal loyalty)
- Both are real, but near-mode better predicts actual behavior

**Hypocrisy or Honesty?**
The book's insight is that the near/far gap isn't hypocrisy in the usual sense. Hypocrisy implies you know you're contradicting yourself. But with near/far switching, each mode is sincere within its context.

You're not lying when you say you value the environment (far mode), and you're not lying when you fly to vacation (near mode). You're expressing different genuine preferences that apply in different contexts. The problem is we don't recognize we're mode-switching.

**Practical Implications:**
Understanding near/far helps explain:
- Why New Year's resolutions fail (made in far mode, executed in near mode)
- Why people don't follow through on commitments (far-mode promises, near-mode obstacles)
- Why we're surprised by our own behavior (we predict using far mode, act in near mode)
- Why abstract agreement doesn't lead to concrete cooperation (different modes)

The book suggests being honest about this: Your far-mode values are what you wish you cared about; your near-mode actions are your [[Revealed Preferences|revealed preferences]]. Both deserve acknowledgment.
