## Definition
Any resistance—cognitive, physical, procedural, or social—that makes an action harder to complete. Friction doesn't just slow things down; below a threshold, it prevents action entirely. Small amounts of friction can eliminate large amounts of behavior.

## Key Insight
Behavior is extremely sensitive to friction. Adding a single extra step to a process can cut completion rates dramatically. Removing a single step can cause dramatic increases. This is the design insight behind every one-click purchase, every pre-filled form, every "are you sure?" dialog. Friction is not neutral—it is a powerful lever.

## How to Apply
Reducing friction for desired behaviors:
- Pre-fill forms and decisions wherever possible
- Remove required steps that don't add value
- Make the desired action the path of least resistance

Adding friction to undesired behaviors:
- Add confirmation steps to impulsive or harmful actions
- Increase the physical or cognitive distance between impulse and action
- Create waiting periods for consequential decisions

Auditing friction:
- Track where in a process drop-off occurs
- Ask: "What is the hardest step? Why does it have to be this hard?"

## Real-World Example
**Charitable giving:** Donation rates drop significantly when donors have to re-enter card details. One-click giving dramatically increases follow-through. The friction of entering payment information—seconds of effort—was the difference between giving and not.

**Medication adherence:** Patients are more likely to take medication when it's stored visibly on the counter rather than in a cabinet. The few extra steps create enough friction to break compliance, especially in low-motivation moments.

**Email unsubscription:** "Reply STOP" is low friction. Finding a settings menu is medium friction. Not including an unsubscribe link at all is deliberate friction—the company is using friction to prevent an action it doesn't want.

**Saving vs. spending:** Automatic savings deductions work because they remove the friction of actively choosing to save each month. Without it, spending is the path of least resistance.

## Common Pitfalls
- Designing systems where the right action requires more effort than the wrong one
- Underestimating how much friction prevents otherwise-willing behavior
- Adding friction everywhere in the name of "being careful" at the cost of completion
- Confusing friction with security—not all friction prevents mistakes; some just prevents action
