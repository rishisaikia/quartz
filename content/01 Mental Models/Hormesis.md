## Definition
A dose-response phenomenon where low doses of something harmful or stressful actually improve health or function, while higher doses are toxic or damaging.

## Key Insight
The dose makes the poison—and the medicine. Small amounts of stress, toxins, or difficulty can trigger beneficial adaptive responses that make you stronger. But there's a sweet spot: too little stress and you atrophy; too much and you break down.

## How to Apply
Apply controlled, beneficial stress:
- Exercise: Small muscle damage triggers growth
- Fasting: Short-term stress improves metabolic health
- Cold exposure: Brief cold strengthens immunity
- Mental challenges: Difficult problems build cognitive capacity

Avoid: chronic, uncontrolled stress (toxic, not beneficial)

## Real-World Example
Vaccines are hormesis: A small, controlled dose of pathogen (or part of it) triggers your immune system to build defenses without causing disease. The stress makes you stronger.

Exercise is hormetic: Lifting weights creates micro-tears in muscle fibers (small damage). Your body overcompensates by building stronger muscles. No damage = no growth. Too much damage = injury.

Radiation: Low-level exposure might trigger DNA repair mechanisms that actually reduce cancer risk slightly. High-level exposure causes cancer and death. The relationship isn't linear—small doses can be beneficial.

Plants produce antioxidants in response to small stresses (UV light, temperature changes). These compounds make the plants—and people who eat them—healthier. Plants grown in perfect conditions without stress produce fewer beneficial compounds.

Cold showers: Brief cold exposure triggers beneficial stress responses (improved circulation, immune boost). Prolonged cold causes hypothermia.

## Common Pitfalls
- Applying too much stress thinking "more is better" (it's not)
- Avoiding all stress and becoming fragile
- Not allowing recovery time between hormetic stressors
- Confusing acute beneficial stress with chronic harmful stress
