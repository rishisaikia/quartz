## Definition
Despite technological ability to share information freely, we actively maintain information asymmetries and ambiguities because many social arrangements depend on them. The resistance to transparency isn't technological—it's social and psychological.

## Key Insight
We could make many hidden motives, secrets, and arrangements transparent, but we collectively choose not to. Information asymmetry isn't a problem to solve—it's often a feature that enables cooperation. Making everything transparent would destroy many functional social arrangements.

## How to Apply
Recognize functional opacity:
- "Why doesn't everyone share this information freely?"
- "What arrangements depend on information asymmetry?"
- "What would break if we made this transparent?"

Respect necessary ambiguity:
- Some things work better when not fully explicit
- Total transparency can destroy trust and cooperation
- Information asymmetry enables plausible deniability

## Real-World Example
**Salary information:**
Companies could easily share everyone's salary publicly. Technology makes this trivial. But they don't, and employees often don't want them to. Why? Because salary differences depend on negotiation, relationships, and factors people don't want publicly justified. Transparency would force explicit justification of every gap, destroying the current system.

**Performance reviews:**
Managers could share completely honest, detailed performance reviews. They don't. Why? Because maintaining some ambiguity preserves working relationships. Full honesty would create constant confrontation. The information asymmetry (you don't know exactly what they think) enables cooperation.

**Gift prices:**
Gift-givers could attach price tags. We deliberately remove them. Why? Because gifts are partly about signaling care and status, and explicit prices make this too obvious. The ambiguity about cost preserves the social fiction of pure generosity.

**Academic peer review:**
Journal reviews could be public and attributed. They're usually anonymous. Why? Because anonymity enables honest criticism. Full transparency would create social costs for negative reviews, reducing quality of feedback. The information asymmetry improves the system.

**Networking and connections:**
Social media could make all your networking calculations visible. It doesn't (or at least we don't use that setting). Why? Because we want to maintain the fiction that friendships are pure rather than strategic. Information asymmetry preserves relationships that transparency would reveal as instrumental.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming all information should be free/transparent
- Not recognizing when opacity serves important functions
- Destroying functional ambiguity by forcing transparency
- Underestimating social costs of information revelation
- Assuming resistance to transparency is always about hiding wrongdoing

## Notes
In [[The Elephant in the Brain]], Robin Hanson and Kevin Simler argue that the open-source/transparency movement misses a crucial point: many social institutions depend on maintaining information asymmetries. While technology makes total transparency possible, we actively resist it because transparency would be socially destructive.

**Not a Technical Problem:**
The book emphasizes this isn't about technological barriers. We have the tools to make everything transparent—public salary databases, open performance reviews, exposed networking strategies, visible wealth displays. We choose not to use them. The question is: why?

The authors' answer: Because many social arrangements depend on plausible deniability, polite fictions, and [[Strategic Ignorance]]. Making everything explicit converts private knowledge into [[Common Knowledge]], which forces social responses that destroy current arrangements.

**Examples from the Book:**

**Education and Signaling:**
We could admit openly that education is largely credentialing and signaling. Students could say "I'm here for the degree, not learning." Employers could say "We use degrees to filter, not to assess knowledge." But making this [[Common Knowledge]] would undermine the system. The information asymmetry—everyone privately knows but doesn't publicly acknowledge—preserves the institution.

**Medicine and Care Signaling:**
We could admit that much healthcare spending is about demonstrating care rather than improving health. Families could say "We're choosing expensive treatment to show we tried, not because it will help." Doctors could say "This procedure is medically futile but socially necessary." But transparency would make the arrangement untenable. Maintaining ambiguity about motives enables both healing and care signaling.

**Charity and Status:**
Donors could admit "I'm giving to signal wealth and compassion, not just to help." Charities could admit "We spend inefficiently on visible programs to satisfy donors." But honesty would destroy charitable giving. The polite fiction that charity is pure altruism enables both helping and status signaling.

**The [[Common Knowledge]] Problem:**
This connects directly to the [[Common Knowledge]] mental model. As long as information remains private or merely mutual knowledge, people can maintain plausible deniability. Once it becomes common knowledge (everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone knows...), it requires social response.

Example: Your boss exploits unpaid interns. You know, your boss knows you know, but it's not [[Common Knowledge]]. You can continue the arrangement. If you publicly call it exploitation, it becomes [[Common Knowledge]], and your boss must either defend it (costly) or change it (costly). The information asymmetry was functional.

**Why We're Collectively Invested:**
The book argues we maintain information asymmetries not because powerful people hide truth from powerless people, but because everyone benefits from certain ambiguities:
- Employees benefit from salary secrecy (lets them negotiate)
- Students benefit from education ambiguity (can claim learning and signaling)
- Patients benefit from medical ambiguity (can show care and seek health)
- We all benefit from not having every motive exposed

This explains resistance to transparency. It's not just elites hiding information—it's collective maintenance of functional fictions.

**When Transparency Helps:**
Hanson and Simler aren't anti-transparency absolutists. They acknowledge transparency can help:
- In corruption and clear wrongdoing
- When information asymmetry enables obvious exploitation
- When the cost of opacity exceeds its benefits

But they caution against naive transparency advocacy that assumes information always wants to be free. Sometimes information wants to stay hidden because hiddenness serves important social functions.

**The Book's Meta-Point:**
Ironically, the book itself violates this principle by making hidden motives explicit. Hanson and Simler acknowledge this: they're making [[Common Knowledge]] what should perhaps remain [[Pluralistic Ignorance]]. But they argue understanding these dynamics, even if uncomfortable, enables more sophisticated social navigation—you can choose when to maintain opacity and when to pursue transparency, rather than naively assuming all information should be free.
