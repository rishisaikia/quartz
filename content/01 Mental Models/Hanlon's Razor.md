## Definition
"Never attribute to malice that which can be adequately explained by stupidity (or incompetence, ignorance, or error)."

## Key Insight
When someone wrongs you or makes a mistake, your first instinct is often to assume ill intent. But most negative outcomes come from mistakes, ignorance, or different priorities—not deliberate harm. Assuming incompetence before malice leads to better relationships and more accurate understanding.

## How to Apply
When something goes wrong:
- First ask: "Could this be a mistake, miscommunication, or incompetence?"
- Consider: "Do they even know this affects me?"
- Only after ruling out simpler explanations: "Could this be intentional?"

Respond to the most charitable explanation first.

## Real-World Example
Your coworker doesn't respond to your important email for three days. You think: "They're deliberately ignoring me because they're threatened by my ideas." Hanlon's Razor suggests: "They're overwhelmed, the email got buried, or they thought someone else would respond." You follow up kindly and discover it went to their spam folder.

A company makes a decision that hurts your department. You assume they're maliciously targeting you. More likely: they made a decision optimizing for different metrics, didn't realize the impact, or made a mistake.

Someone cuts you off in traffic. You rage about aggressive drivers. Reality: they didn't see you, they're having a medical emergency, or they're a bad driver—not someone trying to ruin your day.

## Common Pitfalls
- Using this to excuse genuinely malicious behavior
- Allowing repeated "incompetence" without addressing it
- Becoming naive to real manipulation and bad actors
