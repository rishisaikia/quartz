## Definition
Making many small, incremental improvements across all areas rather than seeking one big breakthrough. The aggregation of marginal gains produces significant overall improvement.

## Key Insight
A 1% improvement in many areas compounds into massive gains. Most people search for the 10x breakthrough and ignore the dozens of 1% improvements available. But 1% better in 100 areas doesn't equal 100% better—it equals exponentially better.

## How to Apply
Instead of asking "What's the one big thing?" ask:
- "Where can I improve by just 1%?"
- "What are 50 small things I could optimize?"
- "What's a tiny improvement I could make today?"

Then systematically work through small improvements in every area.

## Real-World Example
British Cycling was mediocre for decades. New coach Dave Brailsford applied marginal gains: Better bike seats (1% gain), lighter tires (1% gain), better hand washing to reduce illness (1% gain), better pillows for sleep (1% gain), heated shorts for optimal muscle temperature (1% gain). He made hundreds of tiny improvements.

Result: British Cycling dominated the Olympics, winning 60% of gold medals available. Not from one breakthrough, but from the compound effect of hundreds of 1% improvements.

In business: Instead of one marketing campaign hoping for viral growth, improve website speed 1%, email subject lines 1%, checkout process 1%, customer service response time 1%, product quality 1%. Each small gain compounds with others.

## Common Pitfalls
- Using this to avoid making necessary big changes
- Optimizing trivial things while ignoring important problems
- Not tracking improvements to see if they actually matter
- Analysis paralysis from too many potential improvements
