## Definition
A solution that people tend to choose by default in the absence of communication, because it seems natural, special, or relevant to them. Also called a "focal point."

## Key Insight
When people need to coordinate but can't communicate, they don't choose randomly—they gravitate toward options that stand out as obvious, natural, or salient. Understanding Schelling points helps you predict coordination and design systems where people naturally coordinate without explicit rules.

## How to Apply
Ask yourself:
- "If we couldn't communicate, where would we naturally meet?"
- "What's the obvious default everyone would assume?"
- "Which option stands out as special or natural?"

Design coordination by making the desired behavior the Schelling point:
- Make it the most obvious option
- Make it the most natural default
- Make it culturally or contextually salient

## Real-World Example

**The classic experiment**: Two people in New York need to meet but can't communicate. Where do they go? Most choose Grand Central Station at noon. Why? It's famous, central, and noon is a natural time. Grand Central at noon is a Schelling point—it stands out as the obvious choice.

**Meeting on campus**: "Meet me on campus tomorrow" without specifying where. Most people would head to the main library, student center, or most famous landmark—whichever is the Schelling point for that campus.

**Dividing $100**: Two strangers must agree on how to split $100 without communicating. Most people choose 50/50. It's the Schelling point because it's fair, simple, and stands out. Even though 60/40 or 70/30 are possible, 50/50 is focal.

**Standards and conventions**: QWERTY keyboard layout, driving on the right side (in most countries), English as the international business language—these became Schelling points. Once something becomes the obvious default, everyone coordinates around it even without central planning.

**Bitcoin vs other cryptocurrencies**: Bitcoin became the Schelling point for cryptocurrency despite technical limitations. It was first, most famous, most widely known. Even people who think other coins are technically better often hold Bitcoin because it's the coordination point.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming Schelling points are objectively best (they're just most salient)
- Not recognizing when you could create a new Schelling point
- Fighting against established Schelling points without offering a clear alternative
- Underestimating how hard it is to shift coordination once a Schelling point is established
