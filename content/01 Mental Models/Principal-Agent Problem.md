## Definition
When one person (the agent) makes decisions on behalf of another person (the principal), but the agent's interests don't align with the principal's, leading to suboptimal decisions.

## Key Insight
Whenever someone acts on your behalf, their incentives matter more than their intentions. Agents optimize for their own goals, not yours, unless the incentives are carefully aligned. This creates predictable conflicts in business, politics, and relationships.

## How to Apply
When delegating or being represented, ask:
- How does the agent benefit from different outcomes?
- Are their incentives aligned with mine?
- What would they do if no one was watching?
- Can I structure rewards to align our interests?

## Real-World Example
You hire a real estate agent to sell your house. You want the highest price possible. The agent wants a quick sale with minimal effort—they get nearly the same commission whether your house sells for $500,000 or $520,000 (just $600 more for them), but selling it $20,000 higher might take weeks more work. The agent is incentivized to push you toward the lower, faster sale.

A CEO (agent) manages a company for shareholders (principals). The CEO might take excessive risks for short-term stock price gains that boost their bonus, even if it endangers the company's long-term health. The shareholders bear the downside.

A doctor paid per procedure has incentive to recommend more procedures. A doctor on salary has incentive to spend less time with patients. Neither is perfectly aligned with the patient's interests.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming good intentions eliminate misaligned incentives
- Over-monitoring and destroying trust with your agents
- Forgetting you're the agent in some relationships (and acting accordingly)
