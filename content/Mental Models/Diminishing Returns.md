## Definition
As you add more of one input while holding others constant, each additional unit produces less additional output. The first efforts yield the most results; subsequent efforts yield progressively less.

## Key Insight
More isn't always better. The first hour of studying for an exam has huge impact. The tenth hour adds much less. The twentieth hour might add nothing or even hurt. Recognizing when you've hit diminishing returns tells you when to stop, shift focus, or change approach.

## How to Apply
Ask yourself:
- "Am I still getting good returns on additional effort?"
- "Where am I past the point of diminishing returns?"
- "Could this effort be better spent elsewhere?"

Look for the inflection point where more input yields minimal output.

## Real-World Example
You're writing an essay. The first draft takes 2 hours and gets you 80% of the way to a good essay. The second revision takes 2 hours and improves it to 90%. The third revision takes 3 hours and gets you to 92%. The fourth revision takes 4 hours for 93%. You're experiencing severe diminishing returns—spending more time for tiny improvements.

In advertising: Your first $1,000 in ads might reach 10,000 relevant people. The next $1,000 reaches 8,000 new people (some overlap). The next $1,000 reaches 5,000 new people. Each dollar is producing fewer results as you exhaust your audience.

Working out: The first 3 days per week of exercise creates massive health improvements. Going from 3 to 5 days adds some benefit. Going from 5 to 7 days adds very little and risks injury. Elite athletes hit diminishing returns quickly—tiny improvements require enormous additional effort.

## Common Pitfalls
- Continuing to invest in something past the point of good returns
- Giving up too early before reaching the sweet spot
- Not recognizing that diminishing returns means reallocate, not give up entirely
