## Definition
Having something personal at stake in the outcomes of your decisions and advice. Sharing in both the upside and downside of your recommendations or actions.

## Key Insight
People who don't bear the consequences of their decisions make different (usually worse) decisions than those who do. Skin in the game aligns incentives with outcomes and filters out BS. Trust people who have something to lose if they're wrong.

## How to Apply
Ask these questions:
- What does this person lose if they're wrong?
- Do they benefit from the advice they're giving me?
- Am I taking advice from someone who doesn't face the same risks?
- In my own decisions, how can I ensure I face the consequences?

## Real-World Example
A financial advisor recommends high-risk investments that earn them big commissions regardless of whether you make or lose money. They have no skin in the game—they get paid either way. Contrast with an advisor who invests their own money the same way they invest yours.

A CEO makes aggressive bets with shareholder money. If the bets pay off, they get massive bonuses. If the bets fail, they get a golden parachute. Shareholders bear the downside; CEO has asymmetric upside. No real skin in the game.

A restaurant critic who risks their reputation on each review has skin in the game. If they consistently recommend bad restaurants, people stop trusting them. An anonymous internet troll making recommendations has no skin in the game.

Ship captains traditionally went down with their ship—ultimate skin in the game, ensuring they didn't take reckless risks with passengers' lives.

## Common Pitfalls
- Dismissing all advice from people without skin in the game (some is still valuable)
- Creating such punitive consequences that no one takes necessary risks
- Confusing credentials or expertise with skin in the game
