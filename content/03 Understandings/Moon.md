## Origin and Geology of Moon:

The Moon is about one-quarter of Earth in diameter (comparable to the width of Australia). In the Solar System it is the fifth largest satellite, larger than any of the known dwarf planets and the largest (and most massive) satellite of a planetrelative to the planet. It lacks any significant atmosphere, hydrosphere, or magnetic field. Its surface gravity is about one-sixth of Earth's (0.1654 g). Jupiter's moon Io is the only satellite in the Solar System known to have a higher surface gravity and density.

Orbiting Earth at an average distance of 384,400 km or about 30 times Earth's diameter, its gravitational influence very slowly lengthens Earth's day and is the main driver of Earth's tides. The Moon's orbit around Earth has a sidereal period of 27.3 days. During each synodic period of 29.5 days, the amount of visible surface illuminated by the Sun varies from none up to 100%, resulting in lunar phases that form the basis for the months of a lunar calendar. The Moon is tidally locked to Earth, which means that the length of a full rotation of the Moon on its own axis causes its same side (the near side) to always face Earth, and the somewhat longer lunar day is the same as the synodic period. However, 59% of the total lunar surface can be seen from Earth through shifts in perspective due to libration.

The most widely accepted origin explanation posits that the Moon formed 4.51 billion years ago, not long after Earth, out of the debris from a giant impact between the planet and a hypothesized Mars-sized body called Theia. It then receded to a wider orbit because of tidal interaction with the Earth. The near side of the Moon is marked by dark volcanic maria("seas"), which fill the spaces between bright ancient crustal highlands and prominent impact craters. Most of the large impact basins and mare surfaces were in place by the end of the Imbrian period, some three billion years ago. The lunar surface is relatively non-reflective, with a reflectance just slightly brighter than that of worn asphalt. However, because it has a large angular diameter, the full moon is the brightest celestial object in the night sky. The Moon's apparent size is nearly the same as that of the Sun, allowing it to cover the Sun almost completely during a total solar eclipse.

The first artificial object to reach the Moon was the Soviet Union's Luna 2 uncrewed spacecraft in 1959; this was followed by the first successful soft landing by Luna 9 in 1966. The only human lunar missions to date have been those of the United States' Apollo program, which landed twelve men on the surface between 1969 and 1972. These and later uncrewed missions returned lunar rocks that have been used to develop a detailed geological understanding of the Moon's origins, internal structure, and subsequent history.

The post formation cooled lunar surface has been shaped by large and many small impact events, retaining a broadly cratered landscape of all ages, as well as by volcanic activity, producing the prominent lunar mares. Volcanically active until 1.2 billion years ago, most of the Moon's mare basalts erupted during the Imbrian period, 3.3–3.7 billion years ago, though some being as young as 1.2 billion years and some as old as 4.2 billion years. The causes for the eruption of mare basalts, particularly their uneven occurrence on mainly the near-side, like the lunar highlands on the far side, has been an unresolved issue due to differing explanations. One explanation suggests that large meteorites were hitting the Moon in its early history leaving large craters which then were filled with lava. Other explanations suggest processes of lunar volcanism.

Blanketed on top of the Moon's crust is a highly comminuted (broken into ever smaller particles) and impact gardenedmostly gray surface layer called regolith, formed by impact processes. The finer regolith, the lunar soil of silicon dioxideglass, has a texture resembling snow and a scent resembling spent gunpowder. The regolith of older surfaces is generally thicker than for younger surfaces: it varies in thickness from 10–15 m (33–49 ft) in the highlands and 4–5 m (13–16 ft) in the maria. Beneath the finely comminuted regolith layer is the _megaregolith_, a layer of highly fractured bedrock many kilometers thick.

The most commonly known effect of tidal forces are elevated sea levels called ocean tides. While the Moon exerts most of the tidal forces, the Sun also exerts tidal forces and therefore contributes to the tides as much as 40% of the Moon's tidal force; producing in interplay the spring and neap tides. The tides are two bulges in the Earth's oceans, one on the side facing the Moon and the other on the side opposite. As the Earth rotates on its axis, one of the ocean bulges (high tide) is held in place "under" the Moon, while another such tide is opposite. As a result, there are two high tides, and two low tides in about 24 hours. Since the Moon is orbiting the Earth in the same direction of the Earth's rotation, the high tides occur about every 12 hours and 25 minutes; the 25 minutes is due to the Moon's time to orbit the Earth.

## Movement and Phases of the Moon
  
1. The moon appears to rise and set at least 50 minutes late every day throughout the month. This is because:
		1.  Moon revolves around the earth once in 28 days. This means, every day, the moon covers 360*1/29.5 ~ 12 degrees.     
		2.  In the same time, the earth has rotated one full circle, and is back to 0 degrees, i.e. behind moon by 13 degrees.     
		3.  To catch up to the moon, the earth will need to cover another 13 degrees, which it can do in (24*60/360)*12 ~ 50 minutes.
2. The Moon is [[tidally locked]] to Earth, which means that the length of a full rotation of the Moon on its own axis causes its same side the near side to always face Earth, and the somewhat longer lunar day is the same as the [[synodic period]]. 
3. Despite tidal locking, however, 59% of the total lunar surface can be seen from Earth through shifts in perspective due to [[libration]].
4. The moon’s orbital plane is inclined to that of earth’s by around 5 degrees. That is why although the moon comes between the sun and earth once every month, there’s no monthly solar eclipse. Same for lunar eclipse. However, at least twice a year, this does happen. 
    
## Lunar Geography
The geographical features of the moon are categorised into 18 different kinds:
1. **Mare**: Latin for Sea. The main features visible from Earth by the naked eye. They are dark and relatively featureless lunar plains called _maria_(singular _mare_; Latin for "seas", as they were once believed to be filled with water), made from vast solidified pools of ancient basalticlava. Although similar to terrestrial basalts, lunar basalts have more iron and no minerals altered by water. The majority of these lava deposits erupted or flowed into the depressions associated with impact basins. Almost all maria are on the near side of the Moon, and cover 31% of the surface of the near side compared with 2% of the far side. This is likely due to a concentration of heat-producing elements under the crust on the near side, which would have caused the underlying mantle to heat up, partially melt and erupt. Most of the Moon's mare basalts erupted during the Imbrian period (3.3–3.7 billion years ago), though some are as young as 1.2 billion years and as old as 4.2 billion years. Some also believe that many of the lunar maria were formed by giant impacts, with the resulting depression filled by upwelling lava.
		1.  Biggest: Oceanus Procellarum (Ocean of Storm)    
		2.  Mare Frigoris (Sea of Cold)
		3.  Mare Imbrium (Sea of Showers)
		4.  Mare Fecundatatis (Sea of Fertility)
		5.  Mare Tranguilitatis (Sea of Peace)
		6.  Mare Serenitatis (Sea of Serenity)
		7.  Mare Australe (Southern Sea)
		8.  Mare Crisium (Sea of Crisis)    
4. **Lacus**: Latin for Lake. These are smaller basaltic plains of similar origin as Mares. 
		1.  Lacus Varis (Spring)
		2.  Lacus Somniorum (Dreams)
		3.  Lacus Excellentiaea (Excellence
		4.  Lacus Autumni (Autumn)
		5.  Lacus Mortis (Death)
		6.  Lacus Doloris (Sorrow)
		7.  Lacus Oblivionis (Forgetfulness)
5. **Sinus (Bay) and Paludes (Marsh)**: Similar features as Mare and Lacus
		1.  Sinus Medii (Bay of the centre
		2.  Sinus Aestum (Seething Bay)
		3.  Sinus Iridum (Rainbow Bay)    
		4.  Sinus Amoris (Bay of Love)
6. **Craters and Catena**: Crater means "vessel" in Latin, and Catena means "Chain". A major geologic process that has affected the Moon's surface is impact cratering, with craters formed when asteroids and comets collide with the lunar surface. There are estimated to be roughly 300,000 craters wider than 1 km (0.6 mi) on the Moon's near side. The age of large craters is determined by the number of smaller craters contained within it, older craters generally accumulating more small, contained craters.The lack of an atmosphere, weather, and recent geological processes mean that many of these craters are well-preserved. The radiometric ages of impact-melted rocks collected during the Apollo missions cluster between 3.8 and 4.1 billion years old: this has been used to propose a Late Heavy Bombardment period of increased impacts.
		1.  Catena Michelsen
		2.  Catena Leuschner
		3.  Catena Lucrecius
7.  **Vallis**: These are valleys, and are usually named after nearby craters
		1.  Vallis Snellius
		2.  Vallis Planck
		3.  Vallis Schroedinger
8. **Mons**: Mountains 
		1.  Mons Dieter
		2.  Mons Ganau
		3.  Mons Huygen
		4.  Mons Dilip
9. **Montes**: Mountain Ranges
		1.  Montes Rook
		2.  Montes Cordillera
		3.  Montes Haemus
		4.  Montes Caucusus
10. **Dorsa**: Latin for ‘Back’, dorsa are wrinkle ridges found in Lunar Marea
		1.  Dorsum Bucklan
		2.  Dorsum Oppel
		3.  Dorsum Barlow
		4.  Dorsum Smirnov
11. **Promontoria**: Cape or headland in a mare
12. **Rimae**: Lunar Rilles or long, narrow grooves on the surface of the Moon, like channels
13. **Rupes**: Escarpments or Steep slope/ long cliff that forms as a result of faulting or erosion (e.g. Western Ghats in India)
14. **Terrae**: The lighter-colored regions of the Moon are called terrae, or more commonly highlands, because they are higher than most maria. They have been radiometrically dated to having formed 4.4 billion years ago. In contrast to Earth, no major lunar mountains are believed to have formed as a result of tectonic events. Initially, they were considered to be the continental areas between the Marea; as such, they were given opposite names to the mares. Now, however, these names are no longer recognised. 
		1. Terra Vitae
		2. Terra Caloris
		3. Terra Fertilitatis
