
### Galaxies

>[!Info] What are Galaxies?
>Galaxies are vast systems of stars, gas, dust, and other celestial objects held together by gravity, and containing billions to trillions of stars. Most galaxies are between 1000 to 100,000 light-years in radius. Milky Way has a radius of around 50,000 (height would be around 1000) light years, and is home to around 200 billion stars. Even small galaxies, called dwarf galaxies, contain around 10 MM to 100 MM stars. 

Galaxies are classified into different types based on their structures:

1. **Spiral Galaxies**: This is the most common type of galaxy - around 80% of the galaxies visible to us are spiral, and 66% of all spiral galaxies have a bar-like structure (classified as barred spiral galaxies). These have a distinct spiral structure, with arms winding out from a central bulge. The bulge usually contains a supermassive black hole, youngest stars form in gas-rich arms, annd older stars can be found throughout the disk and within the bulge and halo. Both the Milky Way and the Andromeda galaxies are barred spirals. Scientists think the presence of a bar indicates that a galaxy has reached full maturity.

2. **Elliptical Galaxies**: They make up around 10-15% of galaxies within the Virgo Supercluster, a supercluster of that we are a part of. Elliptical galaxies can be completely round to oval, and they don’t have the spiral arms of spiral galaxies. They are often composed of older stars. The largest galaxies are typically huge elliptical galaxies, containing a trillion or even more stars and spanning as much as 1 million light-years across – ten times bigger than the milky way.  Unlike spirals, elliptical galaxies usually contain little gas and dust and show very little organization or structure. The stars orbit around the core in random directions and are generally older than those in spiral galaxies since little of the gas needed to form new stars remains. Scientists think elliptical galaxies originate from collisions and mergers with spirals. The rarest variety of galaxies is the elliptical double-ringed galaxy.

3. **Irregular Galaxies**: Irregular galaxies lack well-defined structures and are rich in gas and dust. They have unusual shapes, like toothpicks, rings, or even little groupings of stars. They range from dwarf irregular galaxies with 100 million times the Sun’s mass to large ones weighing 10 billion solar masses. Astronomers think these galaxies’ odd shapes are sometimes the result of interactions with others. The oldest and farthest galaxy ever discovered is an irregular variety of galaxy known as GN-z11; it's thirty two billion light-years away. 

4. **Lenticular Galaxies**: These galaxies have a structure that combines features of both spiral and elliptical galaxies. These galaxies are a hybrid - they have the central bulge and disk common to spiral galaxies, but without the arms and similar to ellipticals, have older stellar populations and little ongoing star formation. One theory suggests these galaxies are older spirals whose arms have faded. Another proposes that lenticulars formed from mergers of spiral galaxies.

5. **Active Galaxies**: When the centre of a galaxy appears more than 100 times brighter than the combined light of its stars, it's called an active galaxy. Astronomers think this excess energy comes from areas near the galaxies’ central supermassive black holes, which range from hundreds of thousands to billions of times the mass of our Sun. Gas and dust collect around the black hole to form an accretion disk. The black hole’s gravity compresses and heats the disk, which causes the material to glow across multiple wavelengths, from infrared to X-rays. Around 10% of known galaxies are active. They can be spiral, elliptical, or irregular. The Milky Way is not currently an active galaxy. 

	1. **Seyfert Galaxies**: Seyfert galaxies are the most common active galaxies and also exhibit the lowest energies. All Seyferts look like normal galaxies in visible light, but they emit considerable infrared radiation. When observed in the infrared, some reveal bright emission from the donut-shaped torus. Some also emit X-rays. Seyfert galaxies tend to have lower radio luminosities, although some produce radio jets. Scientists divide Seyferts into two classes. Type I Seyfert galaxies display unusual features in their visible light that imply rapid motion near the accretion disk. Type II Seyferts show features that imply much slower motion. However, this distinction may result from different viewing angles into the centers of these galaxies.
	
	 2. **Quasars**: Quasars are the most luminous type of active galaxy. They emit light across the electromagnetic spectrum, produce powerful particle jets, and can radiate thousands of times the energy emitted by a galaxy like the Milky Way. The nearest quasar, called Markarian 231, is located some 600 million light-years away, but we see many more quasars the farther we look. Scientists have identified over 1 million quasars, with the farthest one currently known lying about 13 billion light-years away. Merging galaxies in the young universe may provide the fuel to power the enormous energy output of quasars, but when the feeding frenzy ends, the black hole cannot maintain it. It’s thought that quasar activity may be episodic and that this entire phase may last only about 10 million years.

	3. **Blazars**: Blazars produce light across the electromagnetic spectrum. Their powerful jets point almost directly at Earth, so they appear brighter than other active galaxies. Observatories on Earth can sometimes detect high-energy particles – like neutrinos – produced within the jets and trace them back to their home galaxy. This information gives scientists a glimpse into the environment around the blazar’s supermassive black hole.

Galaxy clusters are large-scale structures in the universe that consist of multiple galaxies bound together by gravity. Each galaxy cluster can contain hundreds to thousands of individual galaxies and are often held together by dark matter.

1. Superclusters: These are massive groupings of galaxy clusters, spanning hundreds of millions of light-years. Superclusters form interconnected filaments and sheets, with large voids of relatively empty space between them.

2. Filaments: These are elongated threads or tendrils of galaxies that connect galaxy clusters and superclusters. They form a web-like structure in the universe.

3. Voids: Voids are vast regions of space that have a significantly lower density of galaxies compared to their surroundings. They exist between filaments and superclusters.

These different structures—galaxies, galaxy clusters, superclusters, filaments, and voids—form the cosmic web, shaping the large-scale distribution of matter in the universe.

### Star Clusters

>[!Info] What are Star Clusters?
>A star cluster is a large group of stars whose members are held together by mutual gravitational attraction. To distinguish star clusters from galaxies (which are also gravitationally bound), keep in mind that galaxies are way more massive. A typical (globular) star cluster contains a mass of 100,000 Suns, while the Milky Way galaxy has nearly 200 to 300 billion stars.

Star clusters are divided into two main types: globular and open ones. The difference between them is significant.

1. **Globular clusters** are large, densely packed groups of stars that are typically spherical in shape. They contain thousands to millions of stars bound together by gravity. These clusters are usually found in the outskirts of galaxies and have an ancient age, typically several billions of years. Globular clusters tend to have a symmetrical distribution of stars and exhibit a higher concentration towards their centers. The most well-known examples are 47 Tucanae and Omega Centauri.

2. **Open clusters**, also known as galactic clusters, are relatively smaller and less dense. They consist of several dozen to a few thousand stars that are loosely held together by gravity. Open clusters are often found within the spiral arms of galaxies, including our Milky Way. They are relatively young, ranging from a few million to a few hundred million years in age. Open clusters have a more irregular shape, and their stars are loosely distributed. Examples are the Pleiades, Hyades, or Beehive Cluster.

In summary, globular clusters are larger, denser, spherical clusters with older stars, while open clusters are smaller, less dense, irregularly shaped clusters with younger stars.

### Nebulae

>[!Info] What are Nebulae?
>Nebulae are vast clouds of gas and dust that exist in space. They are often considered the "birthplaces" or nurseries of stars, as they provide the raw materials from which stars form. Nebulae can be found both within galaxies and as separate entities in intergalactic space. These clouds are primarily composed of gas, dust, and [plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysical_plasma), all of which are remnants of [stellar processes](https://www.thoughtco.com/stellar-nucleosynthesis-2699311), such as the fusion of hydrogen in stars, stellar winds, and supernova explosions. The composition of nebulae can vary greatly depending on their age, location, and other physical conditions. For example, some nebulae may be predominantly composed of hydrogen, while others may contain a significant amount of helium, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen. The gas and dust in nebulae can also be ionized, meaning that they have lost or gained electrons, and this can lead to the emission of light at various wavelengths, resulting in the characteristic colors and patterns seen in nebulae.

Nebulae come in different types based on their composition and appearance:

1. **Emission Nebulae**: These nebulae, also called *stellar nurseries*, are predominantly composed of ionized Hydrogen gas that emits light of various colours; as such, they are also called HII regions. These nebula are formed by gas, dust, and other materials clustering together due to gravity to form denser regions. The density attracts more matter and eventually becomes dense enough to create the formation of stars. The remaining material is then believed to form planets and other planetary system objects. Examples are the incredible formations like the ***Pillars of Creation*** found within the ***Eagle Nebula (M16)*** and the ***Orion Nebula (M42)***.

2. **Reflection Nebulae**: These nebulae scatter and reflect the light of nearby stars, giving them a bluish hue. They don't emit their own light but reflect the light from neighbouring stars.

3. **Dark Nebulae**: These are dense clouds of dust and gas that obscure the light from stars and other objects behind them. They appear as dark patches against the background of bright stars or nebulae. Much like Emission and Reflection Nebulae, Dark Nebulae are sources of infrared emissions, chiefly due to the presence of dust within them. Example of dark nebulae is the Horsehead Nebula, which is part of the Orion B molecular cloud that also contains the emission nebula IC 434, the Flame Nebula (NGC 2024), the reflection nebula [Messier 78](https://www.messier-objects.com/messier-78/), and the nearby McNeil’s Nebula. The dark lanes intersecting the Flame Nebula may be part of the same dark cloud that forms the Horsehead.

4. **Planetary Nebulae**: When early astronomers observed these round, compact nebulae in the night sky – they thought that they must be planets. In reality, planetary nebulae have nothing to do with planets. They are formed when a low-mass star enters the last phase of its life (called the Red Giant phase) and creates dramatic formations of radiating cosmic gas. The star slowly loses its outer layers due to helium flashes from their interior. When the star has lost enough material its temperature increases. In turn, the UV radiation that is emitted ionizes the surrounding material that has been thrown off. The gas is expanding outwards at an incredible speed. Some great examples of planetary nebulae in the night sky include the Ring Nebula (M57), the Dumbbell Nebula (M27), and the Helix Nebula. 

5. **Supernova Remnant**: A supernova remnant is a cosmic explosion that has spread the materials from a star across a huge expanse of space. The remnants of this explosion have formed into a nebula, and this nebula type creates some of the most incredible formations in space. Some nebulae are formed as the result of supernova explosions and are hence classified as Supernova Remnant Nebulae. In this case, short-lived stars experience implosion in their cores and blow off their external layers. This explosion leaves behind a “remnant” in the form of a compact object – i.e. a neutron star – and a cloud of gas and dust that is ionized by the energy of the explosion. The [Veil Nebula](https://astrobackyard.com/ngc-6992-eastern-veil-nebula/) is a prime example of a Supernova Remnant. The Veil Nebula includes several filamentary nebulae, including Pickering’s Triangle.

Nebulae can be part of galaxies, such as the spiral arms of galaxies where star formation occurs. They can also exist as separate objects in interstellar space, often formed by the remnants of supernova explosions or the gravitational collapse of gas and dust. Nebulae play a crucial role in the formation and evolution of stars, as they provide the necessary ingredients and conditions for stellar birth.
### Star Types

Stars start their lives as [clouds of dust and gas](https://www.space.com/orion-nebula). Gravity draws these clouds together. A small protostar forms, powered by the collapsing material. Protostars often form in densely packed clouds of gas and can be challenging to detect. Smaller bodies — with less than 0.08 the sun's mass — cannot reach the stage of nuclear fusion at their core. Instead, they become brown dwarfs, stars that never ignite. But if the body has sufficient mass, the collapsing gas and dust burns hotter, eventually reaching temperatures sufficient to fuse hydrogen into helium. The star turns on and becomes a main sequence star, powered by hydrogen fusion. Fusion produces an outward pressure that balances with the inward pressure caused by gravity, stabilizing the star.

How long a main sequence star lives depends on how massive it is. A higher-mass star may have more material, but it burns through it faster due to higher core temperatures caused by greater gravitational forces. While the sun will spend about 10 billion years on the main sequence, a star 10 times as massive will stick around for only 20 million years. A red dwarf, which is half as massive as the sun, can last 80 to 100 billion years, which is far longer than the universe's age of 13.8 billion years. This long lifetime is one reason red dwarfs are considered to be good sources for planets hosting life, because they are stable for such a long time.

>[!Info] The HR Diagram
>The Hertzsprung-Russell (H-R) Diagram is a graphical representation that helps astronomers understand and classify stars based on their luminosity (brightness) and temperature. It plots the relationship between these two characteristics. In simple terms, the H-R Diagram is like a "stellar family portrait" that shows different types of stars and where they lie based on their properties.
>
>On the H-R Diagram, the horizontal axis represents temperature, with hot stars on the left and cool stars on the right. The vertical axis represents luminosity, with brighter stars at the top and dimmer stars at the bottom.

Here are some different types of stars:

1. **Main Sequence Stars**: These are the most common type of stars, including our Sun. Main sequence stars fuse hydrogen atoms to form helium atoms in their cores. About 90 percent of the stars in the universe, including the sun, are main sequence stars. These stars can range from about a tenth of the mass of the sun to up to 200 times as massive. They undergo nuclear fusion, converting hydrogen into helium in their cores, and their properties are primarily determined by their mass. These stars, including our Sun, occupy a diagonal band that stretches from the top left (hot and luminous) to the bottom right (cool and dim). Main sequence stars are in a stable phase, fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores, and they come in various sizes and temperatures.

2. **Red Giants**: These are large, evolved stars that have exhausted their core hydrogen fuel. They expand and cool, becoming larger and redder than main sequence stars. These stars are larger and more luminous than main sequence stars. They are found in the upper-right portion of the H-R Diagram. Red giants, for example, are cooler but highly luminous.

3. **White Dwarfs:** These are the remnants of low- to medium-mass stars after they have exhausted their nuclear fuel. They are hot and dense, typically about the size of Earth but with a mass comparable to the Sun. These are small, faint stars that have exhausted their nuclear fuel. They are located in the lower-left portion of the diagram. White dwarfs are the remnants of low- to medium-mass stars and have high densities.

4. **Neutron Stars**: These are extremely dense and compact remnants of massive stars that have undergone a supernova explosion. They are composed mainly of neutrons and have strong magnetic fields.

5. **Red Dwarfs**: These are small, cool, and dim stars. They are the most abundant type of star in the universe and have lower mass than the Sun. These stars are small, cool, and dim. They are found in the lower-right corner of the diagram. Red dwarfs are the most common type of star in the universe, but they have lower luminosities compared to other star types.

6. **Blue Giants and Supergiants**: These are massive and luminous stars with very high temperatures. They emit a large amount of ultraviolet radiation and have relatively short lifetimes. They are very hot and extremely bright

7. **Variable Stars**: These are stars that exhibit changes in brightness over time. Examples include Cepheid variables, which pulsate regularly, and eclipsing binaries, where two stars periodically eclipse each other.

These are just a few examples of the diverse types of stars in the universe. The characteristics and behaviour of stars can vary significantly, providing a rich tapestry in the cosmos.

It appears that when stars were first being classified in the 1890s, an astronomer at Harvard named Edward Pickering decided to sort the stars according to how much hydrogen they contained. The ones with the most hydrogen were called A stars, those with a bit less were labeled B stars, and on down the alphabet for a total of 22 classes.  
  
Another Harvard astronomer, Annie Jump Cannon, realized from her observations that the stars could be classified by their temperatures instead, so she ordered the stars from hottest to coldest. The stars labeled "O" in the previous classification turned out to be the hottest stars, and "B" stars were the second hottest, on down to "M." As a scientist, she knew that it is more useful to classify objects by temperature (which explains why a particular quantity of hydrogen is observed) rather than simply the amount of hydrogen present.
