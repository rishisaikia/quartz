## Definition
Situations where the potential upside is much larger than the potential downside, or vice versa. The distribution of possible outcomes is skewed rather than balanced.

## Key Insight
The best decisions aren't about taking less risk—they're about finding situations where you can't lose much but could gain a lot. Seek asymmetric opportunities where downside is capped but upside is large or unlimited.

## How to Apply
Evaluate opportunities by:
- Maximum loss: What's the worst that can happen?
- Maximum gain: What's the best that can happen?
- Probability: How likely are each?
- Seek: Small downside, large upside
- Avoid: Large downside, small upside

## Real-World Example
Positive asymmetry: You meet someone interesting at a conference and ask if they want to grab coffee. Downside: 30 minutes and $5 wasted, slight awkwardness. Upside: A lifelong friendship, business partner, mentor, or spouse. Extremely asymmetric in your favor—always take this bet.

Negative asymmetry: Texting while driving saves 30 seconds. Downside: Death, killing others, jail, lifetime of guilt. Upside: Responding to a text 30 seconds faster. Terrible asymmetry—never take this bet.

Investing in startups: Each investment could go to zero (known downside: 100% of investment), but successful ones can return 100x or more (unlimited upside). This asymmetry is why venture capital works despite most startups failing.

## Common Pitfalls
- Only looking at probabilities, ignoring magnitude of outcomes
- Confusing asymmetry with low probability
- Taking too many "lottery ticket" bets with tiny win chances
