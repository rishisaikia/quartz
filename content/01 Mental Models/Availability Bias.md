## Definition

Overestimating the likelihood of events that are easily recalled or mentally available, usually because they're recent, dramatic, or emotionally charged.

## Key Insight

Your brain judges probability by how easily examples come to mind, not by actual statistical likelihood. Vivid, recent, or emotional events feel more common than they are, while mundane but frequent events are underestimated.

## How to Apply

When assessing risks or making judgments:
- Ask: "Am I remembering this because it's common or because it's memorable?"
- Look for actual statistics rather than relying on what comes to mind
- Notice when media coverage might be distorting your sense of frequency

## Real-World Example

After news coverage of a plane crash, people overestimate the danger of flying and choose to drive instead—even though you're statistically far more likely to die in a car accident. The plane crash is vivid and memorable; the thousands of daily car accidents aren't.

People overestimate the risk of shark attacks, terrorism, and kidnapping by strangers (dramatic, media-covered) while underestimating risks from heart disease, diabetes, and car accidents (common but mundane). More people are killed by vending machines than sharks, but vending machine deaths aren't emotionally salient.

After a friend gets divorced, you might suddenly think divorce is more common than it actually is because the example is freshly available in your mind.

## Common Pitfalls

- Dismissing all emotional reactions as bias (some dangers are real)
- Over-relying on statistics and ignoring genuine warnings
- Assuming you can easily override this bias (it's deeply wired)

## Notes

**Neurological basis**: Availability bias relates to how memory retrieval works in the brain. The hippocampus and associated structures encode memories with varying strengths based on emotional salience, recency, and repetition. Vivid or recent events create stronger neural activation patterns, making them easier to retrieve. When you're estimating probability or frequency, your brain essentially takes a shortcut—it samples from whatever comes to mind most easily rather than conducting an exhaustive search. This is computationally efficient but systematically biased toward memorable events.

**Evolutionary basis**: 
In ancestral environments, a fast heuristic that's right 80% of the time often beat a slow, perfectly accurate analysis. If rustling grass _usually_ meant a predator in your area, assuming it always did kept you alive, even if you sometimes fled from the wind.

Availability bias means we automatically weight recent and emotionally significant experiences more heavily, which makes sense if your environment is relatively stable and recent events predict future ones.

The problem is these Stone Age adaptations now operate in an information-rich modern world where they're often counterproductive—rare events get overweighted due to media coverage, and we can insulate ourselves in echo chambers that confirm our priors.
