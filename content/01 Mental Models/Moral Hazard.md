## Definition
When someone is protected from the consequences of their risky behavior, they take more risks. Being insulated from downside changes behavior in predictable ways.

## Key Insight
People who bear the full consequences of their decisions are careful. People who are protected from consequences become reckless. This isn't about morality—it's about incentives. If you get the upside but someone else bears the downside, you rationally take excessive risks.

## How to Apply
Identify moral hazard:
- "Who benefits if this works?"
- "Who pays if this fails?"
- "Are risk and reward aligned?"

Design better systems:
- Make sure people face consequences of their decisions
- Don't separate risk-taking from risk-bearing
- Use "skin in the game" to align incentives

## Real-World Example
**2008 Financial Crisis**: Banks made risky loans knowing the government would bail them out if things went wrong. They got profits when loans succeeded, taxpayers bore losses when loans failed. This moral hazard encouraged excessive risk-taking that crashed the economy.

**Insurance**: Car insurance creates moral hazard—people drive less carefully when insured because they don't bear full cost of accidents. Health insurance creates moral hazard—people take less care of their health when treatment is covered. (Insurance is still valuable, but this effect is real.)

**Corporate executives**: CEOs with huge bonuses for success but golden parachutes for failure face moral hazard. They rationally take excessive risks—big bets that might pay off hugely for them, but if the bets fail, the company suffers while they're protected.

**Lifeguards at the beach**: Paradoxically, having lifeguards can increase drownings. People swim in more dangerous conditions knowing they'll be rescued. The protection creates moral hazard—riskier behavior.

**Kids learning**: If parents always save their children from consequences (failed tests, lost items, social conflicts), kids never learn to be careful. Protection creates moral hazard that prevents development of responsibility.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming people are reckless by nature (often they're responding rationally to moral hazard)
- Creating systems that separate upside from downside
- Not recognizing when you're the one facing moral hazard
- Over-correcting and removing all safety nets (some protection is necessary)
