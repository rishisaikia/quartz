## Definition
Even if you're better at everything than someone else, you should focus on what you're MOST better at, and let others do what you're LEAST better at. Specialize in your relative advantage, not your absolute advantage.

## Key Insight
It's not about being the best. It's about where your time creates the most value relative to alternatives. Even if you're worse at something than others, you might still have a comparative advantage if they're even better at something else.

## How to Apply
Instead of asking "What am I good at?" ask:
- "Where do I create the most value per hour?"
- "What can only I do, or what am I disproportionately good at?"
- "What should I delegate even if I could do it better?"

Focus on your highest-value activities; delegate or trade for the rest.

## Real-World Example
A lawyer can type faster and better than their assistant. Should they type their own documents? No. The lawyer's time is worth $500/hour doing legal work. The assistant's time costs $25/hour. Even though the lawyer is "better" at typing, their comparative advantage is in legal analysis. Every hour spent typing costs them $475 in lost legal work.

In global trade: Portugal and England both make wine and cloth. Portugal is better at making BOTH. But Portugal is much better at wine than cloth (compared to England). England is less bad at cloth than wine (compared to Portugal). Both countries benefit if Portugal specializes in wine and England in cloth, even though Portugal could make better cloth than England.

A startup founder can design, code, market, and sell. They're good at all of it. But they're uniquely good at vision and fundraising. They should focus there and hire others for design and coding, even if those people aren't as good as the founder.

## Common Pitfalls
- Doing everything yourself because "no one can do it as well as I can"
- Not accounting for the opportunity cost of your time
- Confusing comparative advantage with passion (do what creates most value, not just what you enjoy most)
