## Definition
People tend to follow the actions of others, assuming that if many people are doing something, it must be the right thing to do. We look to the crowd for cues on how to behave.

## Key Insight
We're social creatures who evolved in tribes where following the group increased survival. This instinct remains powerful: we assume the majority is right, even when we have no evidence. This can lead to wisdom (crowds often know something) or disaster (bubbles, fads, groupthink).

## How to Apply
Recognize social proof at work:
- "Best-seller" — Are people buying it because it's good, or because others bought it?
- "Join 10,000 customers" — Does popularity mean quality?
- "Everyone's doing it" — Is the crowd right, or just following each other?

Ask: "If I were the first person encountering this, what would I think?"

## Real-World Example
A restaurant with a line outside attracts more customers ("must be good if it's crowded"), while an empty restaurant next door gets avoided ("must be bad if no one's there"). Often they're equally good—the line just started randomly and became self-reinforcing.

TV shows add laugh tracks because hearing others laugh makes you more likely to laugh and perceive the show as funny. It's social proof in audio form.

In the stock market, everyone buying a stock drives the price up, which attracts more buyers ("everyone's buying, I should too"), creating bubbles. The social proof becomes self-fulfilling until it collapses.

Online reviews create social proof. A product with 1,000 reviews at 4.5 stars sells better than an identical product with 10 reviews at 5 stars. The crowd's opinion feels more trustworthy than the perfect score.

## Common Pitfalls
- Following the crowd off a cliff (bubbles, fads, bad decisions)
- Assuming all social proof is manipulation (sometimes crowds have wisdom)
- Being contrarian just to be different (crowds are sometimes right)
- Not recognizing when you're following social proof unconsciously
