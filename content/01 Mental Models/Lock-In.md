## Definition
A state in which a user, customer, or participant becomes dependent on a product, platform, or system to the point where switching is prohibitively costly—even if better alternatives exist. Lock-in converts initial adoption into long-term captivity.

## Key Insight
Lock-in transfers power from the customer to the supplier over time. The initial offering is often generous—because value during the adoption phase converts to extraction potential once the user is locked in. Understanding lock-in means asking not just "is this good now?" but "what will I be trapped paying later?"

## How to Apply
As a buyer or adopter:
- Evaluate long-run cost of adoption, not just entry price
- Ask: "What does switching cost 3 years from now?"
- Prefer open standards and interoperability where possible
- Negotiate exit terms before you're locked in, not after

As a builder:
- Network effects, data accumulation, and integration depth all increase lock-in
- Deep integrations (API dependencies, workflow integrations) build switching costs legitimately
- Be cautious of lock-in strategies that generate backlash once users feel trapped

## Real-World Example
**Enterprise software:** Companies spend enormous sums on ERP systems (SAP, Oracle). Once implemented, years of customization, staff training, and data stored in proprietary formats make switching effectively impossible. The vendor can raise prices aggressively—the customer has nowhere to go.

**Printers and ink:** Printers are cheap; ink cartridges are expensive. The razor/blade model creates lock-in. Once you have the printer, you're committed to that vendor's ink.

**Social networks:** Your network of connections exists on one platform. Moving platforms means losing access to your existing connections—a massive switching cost that isn't about the software at all.

**Cloud computing:** AWS, Azure, and GCP offer proprietary services that don't transfer cleanly. Once your architecture depends on them, migration is expensive enough to be a credible lock-in moat.

## Common Pitfalls
- Ignoring lock-in risk because the initial price is attractive
- Underestimating how fast integration deepens (and with it, switching costs)
- Assuming lock-in is unethical—it's often just good product design
- Confusing lock-in with loyalty (users may stay while resenting you)
