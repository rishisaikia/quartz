## Definition
The tendency to create coherent stories to explain past events, making them seem more predictable and understandable than they actually were. We impose narrative structure on random or complex events.

## Key Insight
Humans are storytelling machines. We can't help but create narratives that explain why things happened, even when the real cause is randomness or complexity beyond our understanding. These stories feel true and make us overconfident about predicting the future.

## How to Apply
Recognize when you're creating narratives:
- After any event, ask "Am I making this seem more obvious in hindsight?"
- Notice when you explain success with a hero's journey narrative
- Be skeptical of tidy explanations for complex phenomena
- Remember that causation is often messy, not linear

## Real-World Example
After a company succeeds, business books explain exactly why: "They had visionary leadership, executed flawlessly, and understood the market perfectly." After a company fails: "They ignored customers, made strategic errors, and lacked focus." 

In reality, both companies probably made similar decisions—one got lucky timing, favorable regulation, a key hire, or avoided a scandal. We create clean narratives after the fact, ignoring the role of randomness.

The 2008 financial crisis: In hindsight, everyone "knew" it was coming and can explain exactly why. Before it happened, almost no one predicted it. We constructed a narrative afterward that makes it seem inevitable and predictable.

Sports commentary creates narratives: "They won because they wanted it more" or "They lost because of mental weakness." Often, the actual cause is small performance variations or luck, but we need a story.

## Common Pitfalls
- Believing your narratives about why things happened
- Using hindsight narratives to predict the future
- Dismissing all patterns as random (some narratives are accurate)
- Not recognizing narrative fallacy in yourself while seeing it in others
