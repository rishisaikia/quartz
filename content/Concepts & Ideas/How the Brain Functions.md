**System 1**
S1 continually carries out a basic assessment of the world around us and maintains an updated model of our personal world, which represents what is Our Brains as Prediction Machines|normal. Further, It handles skills that we share with animals. Some of these skills are inborn in us - perceiving the world, recognising objects, orienting attention, avoiding loss, and fearing spiders, while some we master through repetition and become almost automatic - associating ideas, reading, social skills. As one becomes skilled in a task, its demand for energy diminishes. All of this knowledge is stored in memory and accessed without much intention and effort by S1. Since S1 is fast, it also takes control in emergencies for quick reaction; however, since System 1 operates with heuristics, it is also susceptible to biases. ^aeb4bd

S1 operates by constructing a model via associations, that link ideas of circumstances, events, actions and outcomes that co-occur with some regularity, either at the same time or within a relatively short interval Into a web of associative memory. As these links are formed and strengthened, the pattern of associated ideas comes to represent the structure of the events in our life, and it determines [[Goal-based concepts|our interpretation of the present]] as well as our [[Our Brains as Prediction Machines|expectations of the future]]. 

When an idea is evoked, it triggers many other associated ideas, which subsequently trigger other associated ideas in a cascade. Only a few of the activated ideas will register in our consciousness, while rest will remain hidden. In that sense, S1 could also be at the root of [[Creativity]]. How deftly a person can bring forth associations basis stimulus might be the essence of creativity. Essentially, creativity is associative memory that works exceptionally well. Sarnoff Mednick created the Remote Association test (RAT) to measure creativity. 

When stimulus is as per what S1's model defines as normal, we are at [[cognitive ease]], and S1 continues to be in control. When S1 assesses more effort is required, it's called [[cognitive strain]], and S2 is engaged. What's key is that a single measure of cognitive ease and strain is connected to a large network of diverse inputs and outputs, which determine our thoughts and actions. 

Because S1 is lazy, seeks simplicity, works basis metaphors (see [[Conceptual System is Metaphorical]], and associations, it exhibits some quirks. For example:

![[Cognitive Ease.jpeg]]

1. *Familiarity*: Prior exposure or familiarity of a particular stimulus leads to cognitive ease, which in turn might lead to assessing the stimulus as more 'truthful'. In other words, repetition might lead to something false to be assessed as the truth. 
2. *Repetition*: Induces cognitive ease and a comforting feeling of familiarity. E.g. Zajonc's newspaper experiment with Turkish words. Same effect also at play when looking at faces. Zajonc argued that this is biological and extends to animals as well. Survival is tough for entities that are not suspicious of novelty. 
2. *Legibility*: Because clear and distinct stimulus induces more cognitive ease as compared to stimulus that's difficult to parse, the former is assessed unsuspectingly, whereas the strain produced by latter engages S2, and is assessed more carefully. E.g. a message that's written clearly is more often believed. E.g. PDF vs. Word doc. 
2. *Priming*: Prior exposure to a stimulus also makes it easier to retrieve related words. E.g. if someone is exposed to the word EAT, and asked to complete the word SO_P, in all likelihood, they'll choose SOUP instead of SOAP. This also explains the Baader Meinhoff Effect, which talks about the increased likelihood of citing a stimulus after having recently encountered it. For e.g. when we learn a new word, we see it being used more often.
3. *Ideomotor Effect*: Prior exposure to a stimulus can also impact a person's actions by association. For e.g., by being exposed to stimulus about old age, etc., people tend to walk slower later on. This has implications on advertising and retail space design. This idea also works in reverse.
4. *[[Causality]]*: Unlike earlier theories that suggested that we infer physical causality from repeated observations of correlations amongst events, Michotte argued that we see causality just as we see color. We are evidently ready from birth to have impressions of causality, which do not depend on reasoning about patterns of causation. This is borne from our need for control over our environment, and the need to suppress randomness. Perceiving patterns and causality gives us a sense of control. Studies also show that when confronted by feelings of lack of control, our pattern-recognition abilities are heightened. This also explains superstitions and belief in the supernatural.
5. *Context (and Coherence) is King*: S1 doesn't like uncertainty. In the presence of uncertainty, it just makes up stuff. Given insufficient information, S1 makes assumptions just to make a coherent interpretation of stimuli. Recent events and current context have the most weight in determining an interpretation. This also leads to confirmation bias ("Is Sam friendly?" Vs. "Is Sam unfriendly?") and Halo effect (which increases the weight of first impressions in a sequence of stimuli, impacting our interpretation of subsequent information). 
6. *Mood*: Mood also has an impact on performance of S1. When people are in a good mood, they are in "cognitive ease", which helps S1 perform better. Essentially, when we are unhappy, we lose our intuition. Hence, cognitive ease is both a cause and a consequence of a pleasant feeling. The impression of congitive ease that comes with the presentation of a cohoerent triad appears to be mildly pleasranble in itself. 
7. *Simplicity*: Simple things are easier to control. Hence we prefer to interpret stimulus in the simplest way possible. This also leads to bigotry.
8. *Reconstruction of Perception*: [[Perception]] is not a faithful representation of the external world, but a reconstruction; it happens inside our brains. It is designed as an efficient way for us to operate in the world. Our visual perception, for example, is dependent on S1's ability to reconstruct a 3D world from 2D using stereoscopic vision or other cues. Similarly, our brain uses hacks like Temporal Synchronization to ensure we can manage stimuli better. While useful at most times, this is also why it sometimes tends to be verifiably wrong, which leads to Optical Illusions and other phenomena such as McGurk Effect, Etc.

Due to the intuitive nature of how System 1 operates and its use of heurestics to arrive at ready associations, coherent narratives and easy answers, in conditions of Cognitive Ease, when System 2 is not called into action, our thinking is susceptible to [[Biases]].

**System 2**
S2 on the other hand is engaged in more involved thinking. One of the common aspects of all system 2 tasks is that they require attention, and are disrupted when attention is taken away. System 2 is also responsible for continuous monitoring of our own behaviour - being polite when angry, being alert when required (see [[Norms]]). System 2 is capable of reasoning and is cautious, but is also lazy. S2 is effortful and requires attention. Activities that require continuous attention, also interfere with each other. Hence, when you go over-budget, you fail. 

**Interaction between System 1 and System 2**
Whenever we are awake, both systems are always running. System 1 runs automatically, while System 2 runs in a low-power mode. System 1 continuously generates impressions, intuitions, intentions and feelings, and if endorsed by System 2, impressions and intuitions turn into beliefs, and impulses and turn into voluntary actions. Usually, System 2 is happy to adopt System 1's suggestions with little or no modification. 

The division of labor between the two systems is designed to minimise effort and optimise performance. If there is a loud noise, System 1 involuntarily allows us to orient towards the source, which immediately mobilises the voluntary attention of System 2. Sophisticated allocation of attention has been honed by evolution. 

System 2 can also have some control over how system 1 operates by programming the normally involuntary functions of attention and memory. For example, when waiting for your uncle at the train station, you can set yourself to scan for a white-haired man, thereby increasing the chances of detection. However, in doing so, we are asking the brain to do something that doesn't come naturally; hence this is effortful and requires continiuous exertion. A crucial ability of System 2 is the adoption of *task sets*. 

Constantly questioning our System 1 suggestions would be impractical as System 2 is much slower and inefficient; it also requires effort. So System 2 can't operate as a substitute for System 1. 

**Effort**
Effort is required for both *self-control* and *cognitive tasks*. The nervous system consumes more glucose when you are engaged in either. 

*Self Control:* One of the tasks of System 2 is to overcome the impulses of System 1 by exerting self-control. For example, in the Muller-Lyer illusion below, Once you measure the two lines, you know that they are the same length. But System 1 continues to perceive them differently. To resist this illusion, System 2 must learn to mistrust System 1's suggestion. However, this exercise in self-control requires effort, depletes energy and is generally unpleasant. This depletion is called '[[Ego-depletion]]'. Unlike cognitive load, ego-deption is at least in part a loss of motivation. After exerting self control in one task, you do not feel like making an effort in another. But strong incentives help.

![[Illusion - Equal Arrows.png]]

*Cognitive Load*: Effort is also required for focused attention, and to maintain several ideas that require separate actions simultaneously in memory, or that needs to be combined according to a rules. Only System 2 can follow these rules since System 1 can only detect simple relationships (x and y are alike, etc.). 

Intense focus on a task can also make people effectively blind (e.g. Invisible Gorilla). This is because while seeing and orienting are automatic tasks of System 1, they still need the allocation of some attention. Similarly, switching between tasks also requires effort, and hence energy.

*Self Control and Cognitive Load together:* People who are simultaneously challenged by a demanding cognitive task and by a temptation are more likely to yield to the temptation, e.g. smoking while under pressure? Basically, System 1 has more influence on behaviour when System 2 is busy. All types of voluntary effort - cognitive, emotional or physical draw partly from the same shared pool of mental energy. 

*[[Flow]] (or Effortless Cognitive Work)*
Cognitive work is not always aversive and people sometimes expend considerable effort for long periods of time without having to exert willpower. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi calls this 'flow'. It's a state of effortless concentration so deep that they lose their sense of time, of themselves, of their problems. Flow neatly separtes the two forms of effort: concentration on the task and the deliberate control of attention.

[[**Intelligence**]]:
Intelligence is the ability to reason, the ability to find relevant material in memory, and to deploy attention when needed. While memory is mostly the domain of System 1, we also have the option to slow down to conduct an active search of memory for all possibly relevant facts. The extent of checking and searching can be influenced by S2.

As per Stanovich, System 2 also has two parts: Algorithmic and Rational. The *Algorithmic* part deals with slow thinking and demanding computation, and some people are better at these tasks; these people are also able to switch between tasks more efficiently, and excel in intelligence tests. The *Rational* part ensures people are more 'engaged', as opposed to being superficial or lazy thinkers; they are essentially more questioning of the System 1 suggestions.

From a S1 perspective, Intelligence isn't about making good decisions as about being good at rationalising decisions. In that sense, S1 is like what we read about in [[Our Mind has No Inner Depth]].
