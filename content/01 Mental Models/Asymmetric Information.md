## Definition
When one party in a transaction has more or better information than the other, creating an imbalance that can lead to market failure or exploitation.

## Key Insight
In any exchange, information is power. When one side knows significantly more, they can extract value from the less-informed party. Markets work best with symmetric information; asymmetry creates opportunities for the informed and traps for the uninformed.

## How to Apply
When you have less information:
- Assume the other party knows more than you
- Ask: "What do they know that I don't?"
- Look for ways to level the information playing field
- Be extra cautious with complex purchases

When you have more information:
- Consider the ethics of using your advantage
- Build trust by sharing information (long-term thinking)

## Real-World Example
Used car market: The seller knows if the car has hidden problems; the buyer doesn't. This asymmetry means buyers assume all used cars might be "lemons" and offer lower prices. Sellers with good cars can't get fair value, so they leave the market, leaving only lemons. This is the "market for lemons" problem.

Health insurance: You know your health status better than the insurance company. If you know you're high-risk, you buy more insurance. If you're low-risk, you skip it. This adverse selection means insurance pools become sicker and more expensive, pricing out healthy people in a death spiral.

A startup founder seeking investment knows way more about the business's problems than potential investors. Smart investors know this and price in a "information asymmetry discount." The founder who hides problems might get funding but eventually get caught; the one who discloses builds trust.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming information asymmetry means someone is being dishonest (sometimes they don't realize the gap)
- Not investing in learning when you're the less-informed party
- Exploiting information advantage in ways that destroy long-term trust
