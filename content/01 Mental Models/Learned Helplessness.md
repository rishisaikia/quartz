## Definition
When someone repeatedly experiences uncontrollable negative events, they learn to believe they're helpless to change their situation—even when opportunities for control appear later.

## Key Insight
Helplessness is learned through experience, not inherent. When your efforts repeatedly fail to change outcomes, your brain learns "nothing I do matters" and stops trying—even in new situations where you actually have control. This creates a vicious cycle: helplessness leads to passivity, which confirms the helplessness.

## How to Apply
Recognize learned helplessness:
- "I've tried everything and nothing works" (Have you really? Or did you stop trying?)
- "That's just how I am" (Is it? Or is it what you've learned?)
- "There's no point trying" (Based on past experience or current reality?)

Break the cycle:
- Start with small, controllable wins
- Attribute success to your actions, not luck
- Change the environment if needed

## Real-World Example
Classic experiment: Dogs given electric shocks they couldn't escape learned to stop trying. Later, when placed in a situation where they COULD escape by jumping a barrier, they didn't even try—they'd learned helplessness. Dogs that hadn't experienced uncontrollable shocks immediately jumped to safety.

Abusive relationships: A person tries repeatedly to fix things, set boundaries, or change the dynamic. Nothing works. Eventually they stop trying and accept the abuse as unchangeable—even though leaving is possible.

Poverty cycles: After years of applications being rejected, efforts failing, and systems not working, people stop trying to improve their situation. They've learned that effort doesn't lead to results. This isn't laziness—it's learned helplessness from rational pattern recognition.

Students who struggle early in math: After repeated failures despite effort, they conclude "I'm just not a math person" and stop trying. This becomes self-fulfilling—not trying guarantees continued failure.

## Common Pitfalls
- Blaming the person for not trying (ignoring that helplessness was learned from real experience)
- Telling someone to "just try harder" without addressing the learned pattern
- Not recognizing learned helplessness in yourself
- Mistaking learned helplessness for laziness or lack of motivation
