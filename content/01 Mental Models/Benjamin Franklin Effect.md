## Definition

**We like people more after we do them a favor — not less.**

Helping someone creates cognitive dissonance that our brain resolves by increasing our positive feelings toward that person.

## The Origin Story

Benjamin Franklin once wanted to win over a rival Pennsylvania legislator who openly disliked him. Instead of trying to please the man or do him favors, Franklin took the counterintuitive approach:

He asked to **borrow a rare book** from the rival's personal library.

The rival agreed. Franklin returned it promptly with a gracious thank-you note. Afterward, the formerly hostile legislator became one of Franklin's good friends and political allies.

Franklin's insight:

> "He that has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another than he whom you yourself have obliged."

## Why It Works

### 1. **Cognitive Dissonance Resolution**

- Our brains hate holding contradictory beliefs
- "I helped this person" + "I dislike this person" = uncomfortable tension
- Brain resolves it: "I must actually like them, or I wouldn't have helped"

### 2. **Self-Perception Theory**

- We infer our own attitudes from observing our behavior
- "Why did I do that favor? I must like them."
- Actions shape attitudes, not just the reverse

### 3. **Effort Justification**

- The more effort we invest, the more we value the recipient
- We retroactively justify our investment with increased affection

## Applications

### Building Relationships

- Ask small favors to warm someone up (not manipulatively, but strategically)
- Let others help you — it bonds them to you
- Follow up with genuine gratitude

### Workplace

- New colleague? Ask them for advice or a small favor early
- Want buy-in? Involve people in helping with the project
- Building team cohesion? Create opportunities for mutual assistance

### Sales & Persuasion

- Get a small "yes" first (foot-in-the-door technique)
- Ask for opinions, recommendations, introductions
- Make the ask reasonable and genuine

### Conflict Resolution

- When rebuilding trust, let the other person do something for you
- Creates investment in the relationship's success
