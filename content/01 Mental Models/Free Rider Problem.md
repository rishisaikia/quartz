## Definition
When individuals can benefit from a shared resource or collective effort without bearing the costs of contributing to it, rational self-interest drives them to free ride. If enough people free ride, the collective good collapses—even though everyone would prefer it to exist.

## Key Insight
Free riding is individually rational and collectively catastrophic. The gap between individual incentives and collective welfare is why many public goods are underprovided and many common resources are overexploited. Solving the free rider problem requires either changing incentives, enforcing contribution, or making cooperation the dominant strategy through iteration or monitoring.

## How to Apply
Identifying free rider risk:
- Is the benefit available to non-contributors?
- Is contribution costly and detectable?
- Is there a game where the dominant strategy is to let others contribute?

Addressing it:
- Make contribution visible and non-contribution socially costly
- Use [[Costly Signaling]] and [[Loyalty and Sacrifice as Commitment Devices]] to filter genuine members
- Design iterated games where reputation penalizes defection
- Use [[Skin in the Game]] to align individual costs with collective outcomes

## Real-World Example
**Public goods:** Everyone benefits from national defense or public roads, whether or not they pay taxes. Without enforcement, individuals would prefer others pay while they enjoy the benefit.

**Open source software:** Thousands of companies use Linux or React without contributing code. A small number of contributors bear the cost while the ecosystem free rides. The projects survive only because contributors find non-monetary value in contributing.

**Group projects:** One or two members carry the load; others contribute little but share the grade. The structure (shared grade, no individual accountability) creates free rider incentives.

**Carbon emissions:** Every country benefits if others reduce emissions. Every country is better off not reducing their own emissions regardless of what others do. Classic free rider structure—which is why international climate agreements are notoriously hard to enforce.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming free riders are morally deficient—they're responding rationally to incentive structures
- Solving free rider problems with pure moralizing rather than structural change
- Underestimating the power of social norms and reputation in solving free rider problems in small groups
- Missing that free riding is only possible when benefits are non-excludable—exclusion often solves the problem
