## Definition
Competition for relative social rank rather than absolute wellbeing. In a status game, your position depends not on how much you have but on how much you have compared to others. Status is inherently positional—someone must be below you for you to be above them.

## Key Insight
Status is zero-sum in a way that wealth isn't. Making everyone richer is possible; making everyone higher-status is not. Much human behavior that looks like wealth-seeking or achievement is actually status-seeking. This distinction matters because the strategies, costs, and solutions are different for zero-sum and positive-sum competition.

## How to Apply
Identifying status games:
- Would you still want this if no one knew you had it?
- Is the value of the goal relative (being better than others) or absolute (having enough)?
- Does the good become less desirable if everyone has it?

Choosing which games to play:
- Status games are exhausting and zero-sum—you can win and still feel behind
- Absolute goods (health, relationships, craft mastery) don't require others to lose
- Opting out of status games you don't care about frees up resources for what matters

## Real-World Example
**Luxury consumption:** A $50,000 watch functions no better than a $500 one for telling time. The value is positional—it signals you're above a threshold others haven't crossed. When status goods become widely affordable, they lose their value (mass-market luxury is an oxymoron).

**Academic credentials:** Grade inflation at elite schools partly reflects status dynamics—students are competing for relative rank, not absolute learning. A B at Harvard means something different than it used to, not because knowledge changed but because rank did.

**Neighborhood housing:** People consistently underestimate how much of house value is positional. When everyone's house appreciates, no one is relatively richer—they're just paying more for the same relative rank.

**Social media metrics:** Follower counts, likes, and engagement rates are pure status metrics. They're valuable because of relative comparison. A million followers meant something different in 2012 than 2024—because rank shifted.

## Common Pitfalls
- Optimizing for status games you don't consciously endorse (career prestige you don't actually value)
- Mistaking positional goods for absolute ones (more money will make me happy—but it often just moves the goalposts)
- Dismissing all status-seeking as shallow (status is real, signals legitimate qualities, and social standing matters)
- Ignoring that some status games create genuine value—competition for excellence can drive real improvement
