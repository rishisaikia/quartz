## Definition
Your conscious mind functions like a White House press secretary—constructing plausible, socially acceptable explanations for decisions made by unconscious processes you don't have full access to.

## Key Insight
We're not lying when we explain our behavior—we genuinely believe our stated reasons. But we're also not telling the whole truth, because our conscious mind doesn't have access to the real decision-making machinery. The press secretary's job isn't accuracy; it's plausible deniability and social acceptability.

## How to Apply
Question your own explanations:
- "Is this why I really did it, or is this the story I'm telling myself?"
- "Do my actions match my stated reasons?"
- "What would my behavior reveal if I couldn't speak?"

Recognize it in others:
- Listen to what people do, not just what they say
- Notice gaps between stated values and actual behavior
- Don't assume people are lying—they might genuinely believe their press secretary

## Real-World Example
You claim you bought an expensive car because it's "reliable and fuel-efficient." Your press secretary genuinely believes this explanation. But you chose a luxury brand when cheaper cars have better reliability ratings. The real reason might be status signaling, but your conscious mind (press secretary) wasn't consulted on that decision—it was told afterward to make it sound reasonable.

A student says they're going to an elite university "for the quality of education." They believe this. But they're also paying $70k/year when they could get similar education elsewhere for $20k. The real decision includes signaling, networking, and prestige—but the press secretary focuses on the socially acceptable explanation.

Someone donates to charity and explains it's "because I care about the cause." They do care—that's genuine. But they also donated publicly, told friends about it, and chose a prestigious charity. The press secretary emphasizes altruism while downplaying status and social approval motives.

You're angry at a coworker and explain "they violated procedure." True, but lots of procedure violations don't make you angry. The real reason might be personal rivalry or threatened status. Your press secretary found the procedurally correct justification.

## Common Pitfalls
- Thinking you're immune to this (you're not—everyone has a press secretary)
- Assuming stated reasons are lies (they're sincere, just incomplete)
- Trying to eliminate the press secretary (it serves useful social functions)
- Not recognizing when your actions contradict your stated motivations
- Using this to be cynical about all explanations (some are accurate)

## Notes
In [[The Elephant in the Brain]], Robin Hanson and Kevin Simler argue that self-deception isn't a bug—it's a feature. Our brains are designed to hide our true motives from ourselves precisely so we can pursue those motives more effectively while maintaining plausible deniability.

The press secretary model explains why we're such poor judges of our own motivations. The conscious mind receives a sanitized briefing about decisions that have already been made elsewhere. It doesn't have access to the full decision-making process—it only knows what it needs to know to construct socially acceptable narratives.

**Why This Design?**
If you consciously knew your selfish motives, you'd be a worse liar. Your face would give you away, your explanations would sound rehearsed, and others would spot the deception. But if you genuinely believe your press secretary's story, you'll be convincing because you're not consciously lying.

**The Dual Process:**
Real decisions happen in the "Oval Office" of your brain—considering status, resources, alliances, and competitive advantage. The press secretary (consciousness) then frames these decisions in terms of principles, values, and socially acceptable reasons. Both stories are real—the decision was made for selfish reasons AND you genuinely care about your stated principles. This isn't contradiction; it's how human minds navigate social reality.

**Evidence from Behavior:**
Hanson and Simler repeatedly show how our actions reveal motives our press secretary doesn't acknowledge. We say education is about learning, but we forget most of what we "learned." We say medicine is about health, but we spend more on dramatic interventions than preventive care. We say politics is about policy, but we're terrible at predicting policy outcomes and don't seem to care.

The press secretary isn't useless—it helps us navigate social situations where explicit acknowledgment of selfish motives would be costly. But understanding this model means taking your own explanations with a grain of salt and paying more attention to revealed preferences (what you actually do) than stated preferences (what you say you value).
