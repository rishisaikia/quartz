## Definition
The tendency to attribute other people's behavior to their character or personality, while attributing our own behavior to situational factors. We explain others by who they are; we explain ourselves by what happened to us.

## Key Insight
The same action gets different explanations depending on who performed it. When someone cuts you off in traffic, you think "what an aggressive driver." When you cut someone off, you think "I was running late and didn't see them." This asymmetry is systematic, not random, and it distorts how we judge people.

## How to Apply
When judging others:
- Ask: "What situational pressures might be causing this behavior?"
- Look for context before making character judgments
- Apply the same charitable interpretation you'd give yourself

When being judged:
- Recognize others are making the same error about you
- Provide context explicitly—people won't invent it for you

## Real-World Example
**Workplace:** An employee misses a deadline. You think "they're unreliable." They think "I was waiting on three other teams, had a system failure, and my manager changed requirements." Both may be true—but the observer attributes it to character, the actor to situation.

**International relations:** Country A builds up military forces. Country B reads this as aggression (character). Country A says it's defensive (situation). Country B's own military buildup is defensive; Country A reads it as aggression. Both sides make the same error in opposite directions.

**Customer service:** A representative is curt and unhelpful. You conclude they're unfriendly. You don't see that they've handled 200 calls, their system crashed twice, and their manager just criticized them publicly.

## Common Pitfalls
- Judging people harshly for behaviors you'd excuse in yourself
- Dismissing someone's explanation as excuse-making when it's often accurate
- Building permanent opinions about people from one observed behavior
- Missing systemic problems because you've attributed them to individual character
