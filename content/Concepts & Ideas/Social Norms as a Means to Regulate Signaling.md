### Key Insight

Social norms regulate signalling. All living organisms signal to compete for resources and status. Signalling can produce extraordinary outcomes such as art, science, and music. Unchecked, it escalates into an arms race that wastes resources and ultimately harms the species. Norms exist to constrain signalling so that competition does not become collectively destructive.

#### Animals vs. Humans

Animals and plants cannot escape signalling arms races because they cannot anticipate long-term outcomes or coordinate restraint. Even if redwoods could impose a collective height limit, a single mutation allowing greater height would capture more sunlight and outcompete others. That advantage would spread, pushing the entire population back into the same costly race. There is no mechanism for species-level restraint.

Humans differ because they can anticipate consequences and coordinate behavior. Through shared rules and standards, norms convert wasteful competition into productive cooperation. Norms reflect what benefits the majority of the group. They are collectively enforced, and violations attract punishment proportional to the severity of the breach.

### Origin of Norms

In forager societies, survival depended entirely on group membership. Exclusion often meant death. These societies were strongly egalitarian, with similar roles and expectations for all members. Norms existed to prevent dominance by individuals or coalitions and to ensure that the group took precedence over any one person. These egalitarian instincts remain deeply embedded in human psychology.

As societies evolved, norms evolved with them. Agriculture introduced property, marriage, inheritance, and organized conflict. Enforcing these new structures required stronger and more formal norms to maintain social order and conformity.

Unlike animals, which avoid conflict due to fear of physical harm or cooperate mainly through direct reciprocity, humans conform to norms because of the threat of punishment by third parties. This capacity for collective enforcement is the defining feature of human norms.

### Enforcement of Norms

Norms are costly to enforce. In modern societies, formal institutions such as laws, courts, and police perform this role. In everyday social life, enforcement occurs through gossip and reputation. Access to mates, status, and political influence depends heavily on reputation, making gossip a powerful regulatory tool. Together, gossip and reputation create strong incentives for norm compliance.

Subtle norms govern behaviors such as bragging, ingratiation, factional politics, and concealed self-interest. These are less explicit but no less influential in shaping conduct.

### Cheating on Norms fuels Growth in Intelligence

Since norms curb competition, they should also hinder growth of intelligence. But they don't because humans continuously indulge in cheating on Norms. This push/ pull is what leads to growth of intelligence.

Cheating is so old and integral that we have evolved a special ability to detect cheaters - when abstract logic puzzles are reframed as cheating scenarios, we are better at solving them.

Similarly, we have also evolved to cheat and get away with it:

1. **By avoiding detection:** One way to get away with cheating is not to be seen. This is why we are highly attuned to the gaze of others. In a crowd, a pair of eyes looking directly at us jumps out. 

2. **By avoiding enforcement:** For violations to be enforced, not only do they need to be known, but they need to be [[Common Knowledge]]. Common knowledge is when everyone knows, and everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on. In a sense, it becomes the official record. In Emperor's New Clothes, everyone knew the king was naked, but everyone didn't know that everyone knew. So a little discretion that ensures our violation is not 'common knowledge' is enough to prevent enforcement. Example: alcohol in brown bag. Enforcement also requires prosecution without formal authority, so our risk-benefit analysis always tells us to let small violations go.

3. **Pretexts**: Pretexts are ready-made excuses or alibis that make proscecution more difficult by having a ready explanation of your innocence. Surrogate advertising, smoke shops selling drug paraphernalia, executives stepping down to "spend more time with family" and hotels "insisting that you reuse towels to protect the environment" are all examples of pretexts. 

4. **Discreet Communication**: The objective of discreet communication is plausible deniability. It's deployed via body language, cryptic communication, symbols, subtlety and informal speech (because it's generally off the record). Discreet communication works by ensuring that the subject matter is not common knowledge, and giving each party an escape clause. *"Want to come up and see my etchings?"* 

5. **Skirting a norm**: Norms are not written down or cast in stone; hence, they leave a little room for interpretation and stretching. 

6. **Self-Deception:** One of the subtler ways in which we violate norms is via [[Self Deception as a Means to Manipulate Others]]. 

### Examples of Norms

**Bragging**

- Achievements should be signaled indirectly, through stories or third-party validation, not explicit self-praise.
    
- Excessive self-promotion is tolerated only when framed as humility, humor, or usefulness to others.
    
- Open bragging is penalized socially even when the achievement is real.
    

**Currying Favor**

- Praise must appear spontaneous, restrained, and justified.
    
- Overt flattery toward superiors is seen as manipulative and reduces trust.
    
- Favor-seeking is acceptable when disguised as loyalty, alignment, or shared values.
    

**Subgroup Politics**

- Loyalty to the group is expected, but explicit faction-building is frowned upon.
    
- Internal disagreements should be handled privately, not publicly.
    
- Openly undermining group consensus leads to exclusion, even if the criticism is valid.
    

**Selfish Motives**

- Self-interest must be masked as collective benefit.
    
- Asking “what’s in it for me” directly is norm-violating.
    
- Actions driven by personal gain are accepted if they can be narrated as duty, fairness, or necessity.
    

**Status Signaling**

- Displays of wealth, intelligence, or power must stay within context-appropriate limits.
    
- Too little signaling risks invisibility; too much triggers backlash.
    
- High-status individuals are expected to downplay their status.
    

**Moral Signaling**

- Moral positions should be expressed with restraint.
    
- Excessive moral posturing signals dominance-seeking rather than virtue.
    
- Silence or understatement often confers higher moral credibility than loud advocacy.
    

**Deference and Disagreement**

- Disagreement must be softened with qualifiers, especially toward higher-status individuals.
    
- Direct contradiction without hedging is treated as aggression.
    
- Respect is signaled through tone, timing, and venue, not just content.
    

**Boundary Enforcement**

- Violations are punished indirectly through distancing, exclusion, or reputation damage.
    
- Direct confrontation is often avoided unless power asymmetries favor the enforcer.
    
- Persistent violators are labeled “difficult” rather than explicitly accused.
    

These norms function to regulate signalling intensity, prevent escalation, and preserve group stability without requiring explicit rules.
