## Definition
The longer something non-perishable has survived, the longer it's likely to continue surviving. Future life expectancy is proportional to current age.

## Key Insight
For ideas, technologies, books, and institutions, time is the best test. Something that's lasted 100 years is likely to last another 100 years. Something that's lasted 1 year might not last another. Longevity reveals what's truly valuable versus temporarily popular.

## How to Apply
When choosing what to learn or invest in:
- Prioritize ideas/books/practices that have survived decades or centuries
- Be skeptical of brand-new trends or fads
- Ask: "Has this survived the test of time?"
- Look for principles, not tactics

## Real-World Example
Books: A novel published 100 years ago that's still read today will likely be read 100 years from now. It's survived multiple generations of competition. A bestseller published last month might be forgotten next month—time hasn't filtered it yet.

The works of Shakespeare (400+ years old) are likely to be read for another 400 years. A viral Twitter trend (3 days old) is unlikely to last another 3 days.

Programming languages: C has been around for 50 years and will likely be around for another 50. A hot new framework from last year might be abandoned next year. Time reveals what's fundamental versus fashionable.

Advice: Stoic philosophy has survived 2,000+ years because the insights are timeless. A productivity hack from a blog post probably won't last 2 years because it's context-specific and superficial.

## Common Pitfalls
- Using this for perishable things (food, people, technology with expiration dates)
- Dismissing all new ideas (some innovations are genuinely better)
- Not recognizing survivorship bias (some old things survived by luck, not quality)
- Becoming stuck in tradition when conditions have fundamentally changed
